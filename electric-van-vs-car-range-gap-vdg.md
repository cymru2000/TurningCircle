Electric vans now trail battery cars on range, efficiency and charging speed by margins wide enough to shape what fleets can realistically buy, according to [new analysis from Vehicle Data Global](https://www.fleetnews.co.uk/news/data-reveals-scale-of-electric-van-range-shortfall-versus-cars) published this week. The company compared the maximum ranges and charging power of 1,053 electric cars and 311 electric light commercial vehicles on sale in the UK, and found the median combined test-cycle range stood at 309.5 miles for cars against 201 miles for vans. Cars also travelled 4.26 miles per kWh against 2.92 for vans, which means a lower electricity cost per mile for a car at the same charging tariff. The average maximum charging speed for electric vans was nearly 26% lower than for cars, as [AM Online's report of the same study](https://www.am-online.com/news/data-reveals-scale-of-range-gap-between-electric-vans-and-cars) confirms.

The gap matters because vans and cars are often bought for overlapping work. A business choosing between a compact electric van and a small electric SUV for urban deliveries is now looking at a 108.5-mile difference in median official range and roughly a 46% shortfall in energy efficiency per kilowatt-hour. At the [Zapmap October average of 82p per kWh](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/charging-price-index) for rapid and ultra-rapid public charging, a van covering 2.92 miles per kWh spends about 28.1p per mile on that tariff, while a car at 4.26 miles per kWh spends about 19.2p, a gap of nearly 9p a mile before VAT and other fees. Over a 10,000-mile year that difference alone is close to £900.

## Why the gap exists

Vehicle Data Global attributes much of it to design priorities rather than neglect. Vans are shaped to carry loads, so they are typically less aerodynamic than cars, and weight matters more because payload is revenue. Adding battery to recover range adds cost and mass, both of which bite harder on a commercial vehicle. Ben Hermer, operations director at Vehicle Data Global, said the differences reflect "different design and use priorities for very different vehicle types" and warned against reading the figures as vans being fundamentally inferior. Rapid charging also matters less for vans that return to a depot overnight, the company said. The [Fleet World write-up of the study](https://fleetworld.co.uk/electric-cars-offer-50-more-range-than-e-vans-new-uk-fleet-data-reveals/) frames the range gap as roughly 50%, which matches the 108.5-mile difference between the two medians.

Real-world factors will move the official numbers further. Vehicle Data Global noted that payloads cause variations compared with the official ranges, and a loaded van on a cold motorway run will do worse than the combined test-cycle figure. That is the same seasonal effect our look at winter range loss in cars covers from the driver's side.

## What it means for fleets

For operators the practical split is between local work and longer routes. Alex Wright, managing director at Shoreham Vehicle Auctions, said smaller electric vans were already working well for local parcel deliveries, where predictable routes and overnight charging make running costs attractive. He cautioned that productivity concerns remain real for larger fleets: delivery businesses run on tight schedules, and any perceived reduction in efficiency can become an issue. Depot charging is the main lever that makes overnight charging affordable, with the government's [Depot Charging Scheme second window of £38 million](https://www.greenfleet.net/news/12112026/second-window-depot-charging-scheme-opens-ps38m-funding) opening on 11 November and the Plug-in Van Grant still cutting up to £5,000 off larger electric vans. The [LCV registration data from SMMT](https://www.smmt.co.uk/vehicle-data/lcv-registrations/) shows diesel still took 79.5% of October van registrations, so the efficiency gap is landing in a market where most buyers have not switched yet.

The pattern sits against the sales picture. [October was the best month on record for electric van registrations](https://www.smmt.co.uk/best-ever-october-for-new-electric-vans-yet-still-just-half-mandated-target/), up 58.0% to 3,369 units and a 12.9% share of the light commercial market, yet the year-to-date share of 11.0% remains less than half the 24% ZEV mandate target for 2026. The range and charging gap is one reason van demand lags the mandate while the car market, with more range and faster charging on offer, pushed battery cars to a 32.0% share of October car registrations, according to the [SMMT's October car release](https://www.smmt.co.uk/year-end-push-lifts-ev-sales-but-mandate-gap-set-to-widen/). The SMMT's own outlook expects the van mandate gap to widen again, reaching 14.9% against a 34% target in 2027 and 18.8% against 46% in 2028. A van that covers fewer miles per kilowatt-hour and charges more slowly will need more of those scarce rapid hours on longer routes, which is why depot-based overnight charging, supported by the Depot Charging Scheme, is the model that fits most of today's electric van demand.
