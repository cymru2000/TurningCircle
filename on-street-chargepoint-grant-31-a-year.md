The Government's grant for households that park on the street has paid out just 31 times in the last 12 months before processing, and only 82 times in total since it launched, according to Department for Transport statistics published on 25 November. The slow uptake sits against a backdrop of around 9 million UK households without access to off-street parking, and comes a week after the RAC called on ministers to end what it terms the "driveway divide" in EV charging.

## The grant and its take-up

The [electric vehicle chargepoint grant for households with on-street parking](https://find-government-grants.service.gov.uk/grants/electric-vehicle-chargepoint-grant-for-households-with-on-street-parking-1) offers £500 towards a home chargepoint for drivers without off-street parking, but only when they are also installing a cross-pavement cable channel, often called an EV gully. The [DfT's grant schemes statistics](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/electric-vehicle-charging-infrastructure-statistics-1-october-2026/grant-schemes-for-electric-vehicle-charging-infrastructure-statistics-1-july-2026), correct as of 1 October 2026 and published on 25 November, show the scheme has funded 82 socket installations in total, of which 44 were in the last 12 months, for £30,778 in grant value. The RAC, which obtained the figures, rounded the latest-year figure to 31 paid out in a year, and the department notes the most recent months are likely undercounted because grants can take months to process.

For scale, the same release shows the [renters and flat owners grant](https://www.gov.uk/electric-vehicle-chargepoint-grant-household) has funded 27,208 sockets since it began, with 6,869 in the last 12 months, and home charging schemes overall have funded 413,420 installations since 2013. The on-street scheme is the smallest strand of the whole programme by two orders of magnitude.

## What the RAC wants changed

In a [press release published on 27 November](https://media.rac.co.uk/rac-calls-on-government-to-end-electric-vehicle-driveway-divide), the RAC said a home chargepoint typically costs around £1,000 and a cross-pavement cable channel adds about £1,300, with some councils charging up to £185 a year in licence fees and others, including Lambeth and Calderdale, not permitting gullies at all. It estimates around 9 million households lack off-street parking, citing RAC Foundation figures, and its research found 22% of people are put off switching to an EV because they have no driveway, while 43% cite long waiting times at public chargers and 29% say public charging is too expensive.

Simon Williams, the RAC's head of policy, said: "The fact that just 31 chargepoint grants were paid out in a year shows that bureaucracy and costs are still a limiting factor for people." He also called for VAT on public charging to be cut from 20% to the 5% rate normally applied to domestic electricity, which the Government temporarily removed from household bills in October for six months while leaving public charging at 20%.

## The policy backdrop

The Government moved in October to make cross-pavement channels easier to install by removing the need for planning permission in many cases in England, though residents still need council authorisation and homes in conservation areas are excluded. It has also allocated a [£25 million fund for English councils](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/apply-for-the-electric-vehicle-ev-pavement-channels-grant) to help households install cable channels. The £500 grant itself is only available until March 2027.

The issue sits directly on top of the cost gap between home and public charging that [we have mapped before](/public-home-price-gap/): home charging at a typical overnight EV tariff costs around 2p a mile, while public rapid charging pays 20% VAT on top of operator prices. Households without a driveway, the group the [charger sharing campaign](/charger-sharing-campaign-9-million-households/) targets with its 9 million figure, are the ones most exposed to that gap, and our [guide to charging without a driveway](/charging-without-a-driveway/) sets out the council-by-council reality of gullies, planning rules and grants. Zapmap's price data puts the weighted average rapid and ultra-rapid tariff at 77p per kWh, against a typical home EV tariff of 8.6p, so a mile on the public network costs many times a mile at home, and the [January price cap piece](/january-2027-price-cap-ev-charging-costs/) shows the cap rise lifting the home unit rate to 32.69p per kWh without touching that public premium.

The registration data points the same way. September's plate-change month saw battery electric registrations rise 36% on 2025 to 99,201 units, a 28.3% share, according to the [SMMT's September release](https://www.smmt.co.uk/record-electric-car-market-powers-bumper-september/), while petrol fell 6.7% to a 37% share and diesel held just 4%. The RAC notes in its release that its Report on Motoring research, carried out before the US/Iran war drove fuel prices up, found 44% of petrol and diesel drivers would seriously consider switching to an EV if fuel prices exceeded £2 a litre, and diesel topped £2 a litre for the first time in October. In other words, demand-side interest is rising at the same time as the cheapest charging route for a third of households stays rationed by a grant that has paid out 82 times.

## What it means for buyers

For anyone without off-street parking weighing up an EV, the arithmetic is now explicit: the £500 grant covers barely a fifth of the roughly £2,300 combined cost the RAC quotes, and with 82 sockets funded in total the scheme has barely registered nationally. The practical route remains checking whether your council permits a cross-pavement channel at all, since a postcode lottery decides that before any grant is claimed, and comparing the installed cost against the public charging prices tracked in [our price gap reporting](/cheaper-week-at-the-plug/) for the years a gully is not an option.
