Volkswagen has capped the charging of about 5,000 UK electric cars at 80% while it inspects batteries that could overheat, and has given owners no date for when the limit will lift. The restriction has been applied by software since the week of 12 October, so the affected ID.3, ID.4 and ID.7 owners have already done almost a month of journeys with a fifth of their battery unusable.

## The UK notice covers three models

Volkswagen's UK notice names the ID.3, ID.4 and ID.7 built between 2022 and 2025, and carries the reference codes 93MV and 93MF ([Volkswagen UK](https://www.volkswagen.co.uk/en/owners-and-services/my-car/battery-recall-campaign.html)). The company says the cars were fitted with high-voltage batteries produced by a specific supplier and that the components inside them need checking against its quality standards.

Autocar reports that the ID.Buzz and the Cupra Born are included in the same action, and puts the UK total at around 5,000 cars out of some 85,000 worldwide ([Autocar, 6 November](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-news/new-cars/5000-electric-volkswagens-hit-80-charge-limit-over-fire-risk)). That global figure includes Audi Q4 e-tron models recalled in other markets, which the UK notice does not list.

## The defect is a fire risk above 80%

The DVSA's entry describes a suspicion that individual components of the high-voltage battery may not meet the manufacturer's standards. The wording of the warning is blunt: "In rare cases when the high-voltage battery is at a high charge level the battery may overheat", and "This creates a risk of fire which could lead to severe or deadly injuries of persons within and outside of the vehicle as well as to significant material damage" ([This is Money, 6 November](https://www.thisismoney.co.uk/money/electriccars/article-16271567/VW-electric-models-recalled-battery-fire.html)).

Volkswagen's own version on its UK owner page is shorter. "When the battery is charged to over 80%, overheating may occur, posing a fire risk."

There have been no confirmed injuries, according to the company, though some property damage has been reported ([Autocar, 6 November](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-news/new-cars/5000-electric-volkswagens-hit-80-charge-limit-over-fire-risk)). Volkswagen has not named the supplier or the battery sizes involved. It says a fault would not be "necessarily recognisable", which is why every car in the campaign has to be checked rather than a sample of them.

## Owners will get a letter, then a workshop visit

Affected owners will receive a letter in due course setting out next steps, which may include a visit to a retailer. Some cars can be checked and updated over the air, while others need a physical inspection that Autocar puts at about an hour, with battery modules replaced where needed ([Autocar, 6 November](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-news/new-cars/5000-electric-volkswagens-hit-80-charge-limit-over-fire-risk)).

Until then the ceiling stays at 80%. Volkswagen has not given a timeframe for completing the checks, and the limit has now been in place for almost four weeks.

## What the missing 20% costs

On an ID.3 the lost capacity works out at between 40 and 70 miles of range depending on the version, calculated from official WLTP figures ([Autocar, 6 November](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-news/new-cars/5000-electric-volkswagens-hit-80-charge-limit-over-fire-risk)). On a long trip that shortfall has to be bought back at a public charger.

At the 4 miles per kWh we used in [our public charging price tracker](/public-charging-price-tracker/), 40 to 70 miles is 10 to 17.5 kWh. At the 80p per kWh that rapid chargers charge pay-as-you-go customers, replacing that energy costs between £8 and £14 for each stop.

For everyday driving the cap may barely register. Volkswagen's own advice is to activate Battery Care Mode, which restricts charging to 80%, as "good practice in all cases". The average UK driver covers just over 6,000 miles a year, about 17 miles a day ([our analysis of the mileage data](/how-far-do-uk-drivers-drive/)), which most owners of these cars can do inside half a battery.

The pinch points are the ones that already test an electric car: a winter motorway run, a full load, a tow bar, a charger that is occupied when you arrive. A car that starts the day at 80% reaches the first stop sooner, and on a route where the next rapid is 40 miles away that margin decides whether the journey runs to plan.

## Volvo's long wait is the precedent

This is not the first UK cap of its kind. In December 2025, 10,365 owners of Volvo EX30 SUVs were told not to charge above 70% because of a battery fire risk, and Volvo recalled the cars in February. Months later many were still waiting for repairs because of delays sourcing parts from China ([This is Money, 6 November](https://www.thisismoney.co.uk/money/electriccars/article-16271567/VW-electric-models-recalled-battery-fire.html)).

The pattern matters more than the difference between 70% and 80%. A software limit is quick to apply and slow to remove, because lifting it depends on a physical inspection and, where a fault is found, on replacement modules reaching dealers. The absence of a date is the part owners should plan around.

## What it means for buyers and sellers

Anyone shopping for a used ID.3, ID.4 or ID.7 has an extra question to ask. The cap is a constraint on what the car can do until the inspection is complete, and a buyer should know whether this particular car is in the campaign and where it stands in the queue.

The DVSA's recall checker at [check-vehicle-recalls.service.gov.uk](https://www.check-vehicle-recalls.service.gov.uk/) shows whether a registration has an outstanding recall against it. A dealer should be able to say whether the battery has been inspected and whether the cap has been removed, and that answer belongs in the paperwork rather than in a conversation at the desk.

Values are the other side, and the effect is not yet clear. Used electric cars have been holding up better than diesel in the trade through the autumn ([our read of the September cap hpi data](/used-ev-september-values/)). A campaign that restricts range for an unknown period is the sort of detail a buyer will ask about, and owners planning to sell should keep the recall letter with the service history. Volkswagen's wider electric programme is under pressure too, with development of the electric Golf paused until the company can fund it ([our report on that decision](/volkswagen-id-golf-development-paused/)).

The practical advice for an owner is unglamorous. Charge to 80% as instructed, keep the letter, book the inspection when it is offered, and do not assume the cap will be gone by Christmas. For a buyer, the recall is a bargaining point and a question to ask, not a reason to walk away from a car that suits the mileage you actually do.
