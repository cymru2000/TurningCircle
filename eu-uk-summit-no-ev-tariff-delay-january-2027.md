Friday's EU-UK summit in Brussels produced agreements on food and drink trade, linked carbon markets and a youth mobility scheme, but it did not touch the rules of origin that decide whether electric cars cross the Channel tariff-free. That leaves the 10% tariff due on 1 January 2027 in place for many battery electric and plug-in hybrid cars and vans traded between the UK and the EU, six weeks from now. The outcome was set out in the [joint statement published by GOV.UK on 20 November](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ukeu-summit-2026-key-documents/uk-eu-summit-2026-joint-statement-html), which commits both sides to cooperate on steel and electric vehicle supply chains under a new economic resilience partnership, with progress reviewed every four months, but announces no change to the Trade and Cooperation Agreement's origin rules.

The numbers behind that decision are stark. EU-built cars accounted for 61.6% of UK battery electric sales and 49% of plug-in hybrid sales in 2025, and industry surveys cited by ACEA and the SMMT suggest fewer than one in four electrified cars and vans traded across the Channel next year will meet the new rules. The associations put the 2027 tariff bill at almost €400 million on UK exports to the EU and €1.4 billion on EU exports to the UK, in their [joint statement of 17 November](https://www.acea.auto/press-release/europes-automakers-urge-united-action-to-avert-punishing-trade-threats-ahead-of-eu-uk-summit/) calling on leaders to act at the summit. We set out that warning, and the factories most exposed, in [our piece ahead of the summit](/eu-uk-summit-rules-of-origin-tariff-warning/).

## What the summit did not decide

The [Retail Motor Industry Federation's policy briefing on the summit](https://rmif.co.uk/nfda-policy-briefing-uk-eu-summit-20-november-2026/), published on 20 November, is blunt about the car trade: no delay to the 10% tariff was announced, and talks are expected to follow. The European Commission is reported to be weighing a one-year delay, or a two-year scheme letting manufacturers offset non-compliant vehicles against compliant ones, and the UK government is reported to support a delay, but any change would need a fresh joint UK-EU decision before 1 January 2027. The same briefing notes that no deal was reached on including UK-built vehicles and parts in the EU's proposed "Made in Europe" rules either.

From 1 January 2027, an electric vehicle needs at least 55% of its value to originate in the UK or EU to trade tariff-free, with battery packs at 70% and cells at 65%, according to the [UK Trade Policy Observatory's briefing on the rules](https://www.uktpo.org/briefing-papers/bp-88/). Vehicles that fall short pay the standard 10% tariff in either direction. The current, looser thresholds were extended once, to 31 December 2026, by a December 2023 decision that said no further postponement should be considered.

## What it means for UK prices

The tariff applies to vehicles that fail the origin test rather than automatically adding 10% to every customer price, so the pass-through to forecourts will depend on how manufacturers absorb the cost. With 61.6% of UK battery electric sales in 2025 built in the EU, even partial pass-through would touch a majority of the electric market. Orders placed now for early 2027 delivery still face uncertainty over final cost, and dealers are advising buyers not to assume the deadline will move. [Fleet News' coverage of the tariff threat](https://www.fleetnews.co.uk/news/electric-cars-and-vans-face-10-tariff-threat-from-january/), published on 18 November, carries the same tariff-bill figures and the same 61.6% EU share of UK battery electric sales.

## The China tariff question left open

The summit also left the UK's position on Chinese-built cars unresolved. The EU has tariffs of up to 45% on Chinese-made EVs and has pressed the UK to align, since it fears Britain could become a back door into the bloc; the UK currently charges the standard 10%. After the summit, the Department for Business and Trade told the [Daily Express, in a story published on 22 November](https://express.co.uk/life-style/cars/2268291/andy-burnham-china-car-tariffs), that the UK had made "no commitments on tariffs" and that decisions would be taken in the national interest. The summit package, the spokesperson added, was about cooperating where it is in mutual interest, including protecting supply chains consistently. We have covered both sides of that argument in [our piece on the Chinese EV tariff question](/tariff-question-chinese-evs/) and [our look at the Made in Europe rules](/eu-made-in-europe/).

## What happens next

The new economic security dialogue agreed at the summit gives the rules of origin a formal venue: steel, electric vehicles and emerging technologies are named priorities, with progress reviewed every four months, according to the [GOV.UK explainer published on 20 November](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ukeu-summit-2026-key-documents/uk-eu-summit-2026-explainer-html). But a political commitment to cooperate is not a tariff exemption, and the joint statement does not amend the TCA. Until a joint decision changes the thresholds, the 1 January 2027 deadline stands, and any delay would have to be agreed before then. For now, the practical advice to buyers is the one the NFDA gives dealers: do not price 2027 deliveries on the assumption that the tariff will be postponed. The SMMT publishes November's new car registration figures on 4 December, and we will cover them in [our regular market coverage](/electric-cars-32-percent-october/).
