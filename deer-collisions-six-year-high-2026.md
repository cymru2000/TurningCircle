The number of deer hit by cars on UK roads has reached its highest level in six years. AA Accident Assist recorded 284 deer-vehicle collisions between 14 September and 13 November 2026, the peak of the autumn rutting season, up 4% on the 273 incidents in the same period last year and up 46% on the 195 recorded in 2021, according to [figures published by the AA on 26 November](https://www.theaa.com/about-us/newsroom/deer-collisions-six-year-high). The warning is not limited to the rutting window: the same service logged 1,106 deer collisions between January and August 2026, a pace that puts the full year near 1,400 attended incidents. The AA itself is careful to say its figures are only a snapshot of the national picture.

The wider context is starker still. The British Deer Society estimates that more than 40,000 deer are killed or seriously injured on UK roads every year, and says the true figure may be nearer 74,000, because there is no legal requirement to report hitting a deer and no central collation of deer-related crashes. When an animal is logged as a hazard in a reported injury collision, the police-recorded numbers are far smaller: analysis of Department for Transport figures by [Volvo Car UK, published on 22 October 2026](https://volvocars.com/uk/media/press-releases/08CA9B264B577E40), counted 1,503 animal-related injury collisions in Great Britain between 2021 and 2025, injuring or killing 1,939 people.

## The numbers behind the warning

The AA's rutting-season series, which covers 14 September to 13 November each year, now reads 195 in 2021, 222 in 2022, 242 in 2023, 276 in 2024, 273 in 2025 and 284 in 2026. The rise over five years is almost half as much again, and the 2026 figure is the highest in the six-year series. The AA's own footnote is explicit that its numbers represent incidents recorded by AA Accident Assist and not the total across the UK, which is why the British Deer Society's estimate of over 40,000 deer killed or injured on UK roads every year, and possibly nearer 74,000, is the better guide to the national scale. Even the police-recorded injury-collision figures sit far below that estimate: the [Volvo Car UK analysis of Department for Transport data](https://volvocars.com/uk/media/press-releases/08CA9B264B577E40) counted just 1,503 animal-related injury collisions in Great Britain across 2021 to 2025.

## Why autumn and winter are the danger window

Deer are most active on roads at this time of year. The rutting season drives more movement, with fallow deer particularly active from late September to early November and October the peak month. Charles Smith-Jones, technical adviser at the British Deer Society, said shorter days mean deer move during periods of reduced visibility, particularly at dawn and dusk, and sometimes throughout the day. "If you see one deer, expect others nearby and reduce your speed accordingly," he said. "There is no absolutely safe period."

Darkness is where the risk concentrates. Volvo's analysis of the DfT data found that 54% of animal-related injury collisions between 2021 and 2025 happened in darkness, against 28% of all injury collisions, and that 79% took place on rural roads. Almost three in 10 of the animal-related collisions happened between 7pm and midnight. Those figures matter more from now on, as the clocks have gone back and most evening journeys now happen in the dark, a point the site has covered before in [our look at winter driving and range myths](/winter-ev-range-myths/).

## The human cost

The police-recorded figures are a fraction of the estimated deer toll, but they show what these collisions do to people. Across 2021 to 2025, the 1,503 animal-related injury collisions produced 1,939 casualties: 439 in 2021 from 348 collisions, 452 from 339 in 2022, 360 from 280 in 2023, 347 from 265 in 2024 and 341 from 271 in 2025. Around 28% of those collisions were classified as fatal or serious. The annual collision count has fallen from 348 in 2021 to 271 in 2025, but the casualty count has not fallen in step, which means the average severity per collision has risen.

For context against other road risks, the same DfT dataset records tens of thousands of injury collisions every year, and [the DVSA's MOT figures show that vehicle condition is a separate, measurable risk](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistical-data-sets/dvsa-mot-statistics) that drivers can check themselves before a winter journey, much as our [MOT failure breakdown for 2025-26](/what-actually-fails-an-mot-2025-26/) sets out.

## What to do if a deer steps out

Tim Rankin, managing director of AA Accident Assist, urged drivers to reduce their speed in areas where deer may be present and to remember that where there is one deer, others may follow. "If a deer suddenly appears in front of your vehicle, brake firmly while maintaining control," he said. "Swerving to avoid an animal could result in a far more serious collision." His warning also covers the cost: collisions can cause serious injuries and thousands of pounds of damage to vehicles.

Practical habits follow from that. On rural roads and routes through woodland, keep to lower speeds at dawn and dusk, use dipped headlights properly after dark, and scan the verges rather than the centre of the road. If a collision does happen, report it: the British Deer Society's Deer Map app lets drivers record location, date and species, which is how the national picture gets built given that police figures capture only a minority of incidents.
