From Saturday, any new car model seeking type approval in the European Union must meet the Euro 7 emissions standard. The date lands four days from now, and it has already ended some model lines: the Honda Civic Type R and Audi RS 3 are among the cars withdrawn rather than re-engineered to comply ([Auto Express, 24 November](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/news/370993/euro-7-car-emissions-standard-force-next-week-what-does-it-mean-uk-motorists)). For British buyers the practical change is smaller than the headlines suggest, because the Government decided in October not to copy the 2026 date. Great Britain will require Euro 7 for all new cars and vans registered from 29 November 2027 instead ([DfT government response, 27 October](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/updating-the-minimum-emission-standard-for-new-road-vehicles/outcome/updating-the-minimum-emission-standard-for-new-road-vehicles-government-response)).

## What Euro 7 actually changes

Euro 7 is Regulation (EU) 2024/1257, and its timetable has two stages: new vehicle types from 29 November 2026, and all new cars and vans registered from 29 November 2027 ([EUR-Lex summary of Regulation (EU) 2024/1257](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/summary/vehicle-emissions-and-battery-durability-euro-7-technical-requirements-and-certification-rules.html)). The exhaust limits for cars barely move from Euro 6e: petrol cars stay at 0.06g/km of nitrogen oxides and diesel at 0.08g/km. The real differences are elsewhere. Brake and tyre wear particles are limited for the first time, at 7mg per kilometre for petrol, diesel and hybrid cars against 3mg for electric cars, which use regenerative braking ([Auto Express, 24 November](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/news/370993/euro-7-car-emissions-standard-force-next-week-what-does-it-mean-uk-motorists)). Cars must also be tested in real driving conditions, including temperatures up to 45 degrees Celsius and short stop-start trips. Emissions control systems must stay effective for 10 years or 200,000km, double the previous durability requirement, and on-board monitoring must warn the driver when emissions exceed legal limits. Particle number limits move to the PN10 scale, bringing particles above 10 nanometres into scope for the first time ([DfT consultation on the minimum emission standard](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/updating-the-minimum-emission-standard-for-new-road-vehicles/updating-the-minimum-emission-standard-for-new-road-vehicles)).

The battery rule matters most for used car buyers. From the standard taking full effect, electric and plug-in hybrid cars must retain at least 80% of original battery capacity after five years or 100,000km, and at least 72% after eight years or 160,000km, with a standardised battery health monitor fitted so the figure is readable ([Autocar, 9 November](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-news/technology/new-emissions-rules-month-target-brake-dust-and-tyre-wear)). That standardises the information a second-hand EV buyer sees, which is exactly the gap our earlier battery health coverage looked at ([how long EV batteries really last](/battery-health-longevity/)).

## Why Britain is not using the 2026 date

The Department for Transport consulted on copying the EU timetable in April 2026, but manufacturers told it the 29 November 2026 date for new types left too little lead time to update model lines and did not fit product planning cycles. DfT dropped that date and kept 29 November 2027 for all new unlimited-volume light-duty registrations in Great Britain, with small-volume manufacturers (fewer than 2,500 cars or vans a year in GB) getting until 1 July 2030 ([DfT government response, 27 October](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/updating-the-minimum-emission-standard-for-new-road-vehicles/outcome/updating-the-minimum-emission-standard-for-new-road-vehicles-government-response)). We covered the decision and the battery test when it was announced ([Euro 7 confirmed for 2027](/euro-7-confirmed-2027-battery-rules/)).

There is a legal catch in the meantime. Until the statutory instrument under the Product Regulation and Metrology Act 2025 comes into force, the Vehicle Certification Agency cannot accept EU Euro 7 type approvals, including temporary Euro 7-TEMP approvals, as evidence of compliance with the GB Whole Vehicle Type Approval framework. Manufacturers seeking GB approval close to the EU date are being told to rely on UNECE approvals instead, with later series such as R83.09, R154.04 and R168.01 accepted as equivalent ([DfT government response, 27 October](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/updating-the-minimum-emission-standard-for-new-road-vehicles/outcome/updating-the-minimum-emission-standard-for-new-road-vehicles-government-response)). The same 29 November 2026 date also brings in a separate administrative requirement: electronic Certificates of Conformity for newly manufactured vehicles covered by GB or UKNI type approval ([VCA eCoC guidance](https://www.vehicle-certification-agency.gov.uk/electronic-certificates-of-conformity-ecocs/)). That is a data requirement, not an emissions one. Northern Ireland follows the EU timetable throughout, so the 2026 new-types date does apply there.

## What it means for a buyer this winter

A car registered this week faces no Euro 7 requirement, and cars already on the road are untouched by the standard entirely. Models already on sale have until 29 November 2027 to be adjusted or withdrawn, which is the window behind the Civic Type R and RS 3 exits ([Auto Express, 24 November](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/news/370993/euro-7-car-emissions-standard-force-next-week-what-does-it-mean-uk-motorists)). The withdrawals are not limited to those two: Honda's refreshed Civic Type R for 2027 is being kept out of UK and European showrooms because it cannot pass Euro 7, and Mercedes has already ended the A 45 S, leaving the upper hot hatchback class thinning out ([Auto Express, 2 October](https://autoexpress.co.uk/honda/civic/370577/honda-civic-type-r-refreshed-2027-uk-cant-have-it)). The longer-run question is whether clean-air zones follow the standard. London's ULEZ currently uses Euro 6 as its baseline for petrol cars, and there is no decision to move it to Euro 7, but the direction of travel on non-exhaust emissions suggests the pressure will build ([Auto Express, 24 November](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/news/370993/euro-7-car-emissions-standard-force-next-week-what-does-it-mean-uk-motorists)). For anyone buying a used EV in the next two years, the 80% battery floor arriving with Euro 7 gives a legal backstop the market does not have today, and links to our guide on [what to check before buying a used EV](/before-you-buy-used-ev/).
