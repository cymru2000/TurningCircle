Ofgem has set the energy price cap for 1 January to 31 March 2027 at 26% above the current level, and the electricity rate on a standard variable tariff rises from 26.32p to 32.69p per kWh. For the roughly two thirds of UK EV drivers who charge at home, that is the number that decides what a mile costs from New Year. The arithmetic is less alarming than the headline: at 32.69p per kWh, a car using 25kWh per 100 miles costs about 8.2p a mile, against roughly 16.0p a mile for a 45mpg petrol car at the current average pump price of 161.96p a litre.

The cap covers around 20 million households on standard variable tariffs; the roughly 10 million on fixed deals are not affected while their contract lasts. The Government has also said around 20 million households will receive a £100 payment towards their energy bill from January.

## What the new rates are

The [Ofgem announcement of 25 November](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/press-release/energy-price-cap-rise-26-january-2027) puts the typical dual-fuel Direct Debit bill at £2,166 a year, up from £1,723, if the January level was sustained for a year. Underneath that headline, the [unit rates and standing charges page](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/your-energy-supply/your-energy-bill/energy-price-cap-unit-rates-and-standing-charges) gives the Great Britain average Direct Debit electricity rate as 32.69p per kWh from 26.32p, a rise of 24.2%, with the daily standing charge essentially flat at 54.77p against 54.83p. Gas rises faster, from 7.97p to 10.96p per kWh, an increase of 37.5%, which is why the dual-fuel bill moves more than the electricity rate alone. VAT on household electricity is 0% for the cap period to 30 June 2027, while gas stays at 5%, so the electricity figures carry no VAT.

Ofgem attributes the rise to the continued conflict in the Middle East pushing up international gas prices. Neil Kenward, Ofgem's Director General for Markets, said the increase "will be a worry for many households as winter approaches" and urged anyone struggling to contact their supplier for support.

## What a full charge will cost

Applying the new rate to a usable 60kWh battery, a full charge from empty costs £19.61, up from £15.79 under the October to December cap, a rise of £3.82. [Auto Express's table of model-by-model costs](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/news/370323/electric-cars-get-ps9-more-expensive-charge-home-under-new-energy-price-cap), published on 25 November, runs from about £2 more for the smallest batteries to £8.98 more for a 141kWh BMW iX5, whose full charge moves from £37.11 to £46.09. Those are worst-case figures for drivers on a standard variable tariff with no EV-specific deal.

Drivers on an off-peak EV tariff are insulated. Auto Express puts a typical off-peak rate at 8p per kWh; even if that rose by the same 26% to about 10p, a 60kWh full charge would rise by only £1.20, from £4.80 to £6.00. At 8p per kWh the same car costs about 2.0p a mile, which is why we have covered the tariff question before in [our look at the October cap](/october-price-cap-day-one/) and [the two-tier cost of driving electric](/public-home-price-gap/).

## The cost per mile, set against petrol

The [DESNZ weekly road fuel prices for the week commencing 23 November](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/weekly-road-fuel-prices), published on 24 November, put average unleaded petrol at 161.96p a litre and diesel at 193.29p. A 45mpg petrol car therefore costs about 16.0p a mile in fuel, and a 45mpg diesel about 19.1p a mile. The same 25kWh per 100 miles at 32.69p per kWh gives 8.2p a mile for the EV, so home charging at the new cap still costs about half as much per mile as petrol, and less than half of diesel.

Over 10,000 miles a year, that is roughly £817 of electricity against about £1,603 of petrol for the 45mpg car, a saving of around £786. The gap has been widening for months: pump prices have fallen for five consecutive weeks while the January cap rise is already fixed, and the [SMMT's October registration data](https://www.smmt.co.uk/vehicle-data/car-registrations/) shows battery electric cars taking a 32.0% share of the market as buyers respond to exactly this running-cost difference. Used-market data points the same way, with [electric vans now selling at auction in 11.1 days](/used-electric-van-auction-surge-november-2026/) partly on fuel-price savings.

## Why the rise is happening now

Ofgem sets the wholesale part of the cap from market prices over an observation window before each announcement, so the January figure reflects gas prices that rose through the autumn, not the price on announcement day. The regulator says gas is rising faster than electricity because of the growing shift of electricity generation away from gas-fired power stations towards renewables. The [BBC's report of the announcement](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cm20qnq33e90o) notes the cap will be at its highest level since the winter of 2022 to 2023 once the previous Energy Price Guarantee period is set aside.

The £100 bill payment announced by the Government applies from January and is separate from the cap itself. The cap limits unit rates and standing charges on default tariffs; it does not cap the total bill, so actual costs depend on how much energy a household uses.

## What drivers can do before 1 January

The first check is which tariff the household is actually on. Around 10 million households on fixed deals keep their current rates until those deals end, so there is nothing to change. For anyone on a standard variable tariff, switching to a time-of-use EV tariff with a cheap overnight rate is the single most effective move: at 8p per kWh the same 60kWh full charge costs £4.80, and the mile costs about 2.0p rather than 8.2p. Public charging is unaffected by this announcement in one respect: it still carries 20% VAT, which is why [the gap between home and public charging](/cheaper-week-at-the-plug/) remains the widest it has been.
