For most of the electric transition, the word "affordable" at the bottom of a new car price list has meant £30,000. That has changed this autumn. The £3,750 Electric Car Grant has reshaped the bottom of the market, the first true city cars are on the way, and the cheapest new electric cars in Britain now start within reach of a well-specced petrol supermini. This is what the affordable shelf looks like right now, and where the compromises sit.

## What the grant does to prices

First, the mechanics. The Electric Car Grant takes [£3,750 off the price of cars in its top band and £1,500 off band 2, for cars with a list price of up to £37,000](https://edie.net/budget-chancellor-confirms-pay-per-mile-ev-tax-and-funding-boost-for-chargers). The November 2025 Budget extended the scheme to 2030 with £2bn of total funding, so this is not a short window that will slam shut while you are still comparing test drives.

Renault has gone further than most and simply baked the grant into its advertised prices. UK orders opened this week for the updated Renault 5 E-Tech, with [the 40kWh entry version unchanged at £21,495 OTR and the 52kWh Comfort Range at £23,945, both including the £3,750 grant](https://electrive.com/2026/09/24/uk-orders-open-for-updated-renault-5-e-tech/). The update is not cosmetic: the small battery now manages 196 miles on the WLTP cycle, up from 312km (194 miles) before, and the larger one reaches 267 miles. The numbers rose because of aerodynamic work and battery electronics rather than a bigger pack, which is the efficient way to buy range.

The 5 is not alone. Autocar maintains a running list of grant-eligible cars, and the affordable end now includes [the Kia EV2 from £24,245 after grant, the Nissan Micra 52kWh at £23,245 and the Vauxhall Frontera Electric from £22,495](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-news/electric-cars/all-cars-eligible-uks-electric-car-grant), among more than 45 models. Prices shift as manufacturers adjust list prices to qualify, so treat every figure as accurate at the time of writing and check the current list before you commit.

## The city car tier is coming

Beneath the superminis, a proper city car tier is forming. The Renault 5's little sibling, the Twingo-based Nissan Pixo, was revealed on 23 September: [a 27.5kWh LFP battery, a 60kW motor, up to 259km (161 miles) of range and 50kW DC charging, with deliveries from early 2027](https://www.electrive.com/2026/09/23/nissan-pixo-an-electric-return-to-the-a-segment/). It will be Nissan's first model in Europe with an LFP battery, the cheaper lithium chemistry that trades some range for cost and durability.

Nissan has not confirmed a UK price. What it has said is that prices, equipment and pre-order details will be announced from December onwards, and its French sister model starts under €20,000. Expect the Pixo to arrive below £20,000, treat that as an expectation rather than a fact, and read our [first look at the Pixo](/nissan-pixo-revealed/) for the full detail.

## What your money actually buys

The honest summary of the shelf in late 2026 looks like this. Around £21,000 to £24,000 buys 195 to 270 miles of WLTP range in a supermini, with AC charging at home and DC top-ups at 50kW to 100kW or so. Around £22,500 to £27,000 buys small-crossover practicality with similar range. Below £20,000, arriving from early 2027, you get a genuine city car with roughly 160 miles.

The pattern is clear: the affordable cars cluster at 150 to 250 miles of range, which covers typical daily driving with a comfortable margin and suits one-car households for all but the longest trips. If you genuinely need 300 miles and rapid charging on long journeys as a routine, that still means a bigger battery and a £30,000-plus price tag, and no grant currently changes that.

The compromises sit in three places. Charging speed is the first: most of these cars top out far below the 150kW to 250kW rates of bigger EVs, which matters mainly on motorway stops. Rear space and boot size are the second, because small footprints are what make them cheap to run and buy. Equipment is the third, though the gap has narrowed: the Pixo ships with adaptive cruise control, lane-keeping assist and a 10-inch touchscreen, none of which would have been found at the bottom of any price list two years ago.

## Who the cheap shelf suits

This tier suits the driver the EV market struggled to reach for years: someone who parks where they can charge, mostly drives locally, and does not want to spend £35,000 to stop buying petrol. If you charge at home, a small battery is not a limitation so much as a smaller bill, and our guide to [small EVs and city driving](/small-evs-city-driving/) covers how the daily maths works out. If you are still weighing up whether a wallbox makes sense at your place, start with [whether you actually need a 7kW wallbox](/do-you-need-a-7kw-wallbox/) before spending anything.

Where does the affordable shelf go from here? More models, not fewer: the grant runs to 2030, the Pixo and its platform siblings arrive from early 2027, and manufacturers are actively cutting list prices to squeeze under the £37,000 cap. The £20,000 new electric car has gone from a rumour to a date on the calendar. Our [autumn grant briefing](/grants-autumn-update/) tracks the list as it grows.
