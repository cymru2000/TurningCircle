There is no rulebook for public charging. Nothing in the driving test covers it, no regulation dictates who gets the last rapid charger at a busy motorway services on a Friday evening, and no signage explains the conventions that every experienced electric car driver has quietly absorbed. Petrol forecourts had decades to settle their norms: pump on the left, pay at the kiosk, move your car before you browse the meal deal. Electric charging is still working its norms out, and the process is happening in public, one bay at a time.

The stakes are higher than they look, because the public rapid network is a shared resource used intensely at specific moments rather than steadily through the year. How people behave at the charger decides how well that resource stretches.

## How the network is actually used

Start with the usage pattern, since it explains why etiquette carries so much weight. Zapmap's annual EV charging survey, the largest of its kind in the UK with 3,976 fully electric drivers responding, found that [49% of EV drivers use the public network less than once a month](https://www.zapmap.com/news/ev-charging-survey-2025-results). Of those, more than half, around 28% of all respondents, charge publicly only during specific periods such as summer holidays or the festive season. For most drivers, most of the time, home charging does the daily work, and the public network is a long-distance tool.

That pattern concentrates demand ferociously. The same survey charted the rise of dedicated charging hubs, which have [more than doubled in use over three years, from 34% of drivers in 2022 to 70% in 2025](https://www.zapmap.com/news/ev-charging-survey-2025-results), overtaking motorway service areas as the most popular place to charge. On a bank holiday weekend, the entire year's worth of occasional users arrives at once, and the difference between a smooth stop and a frustrating queue often comes down to how the people ahead of you behaved.

The backdrop is improving: driver satisfaction with the public charging experience reached [69 out of 100 in the 2025 survey, up five points on the year before](https://www.zapmap.com/news/ev-charging-survey-2025-results), with 60% of drivers saying the network has visibly improved. The infrastructure behind it is growing quickly too: Zapmap's industry review counted [nearly 9,759 ultra-rapid charge points of 150kW or more by the end of November 2025, up over 46% year on year](https://www.zapmap.com/news/ev-industry-review-2025-outlook-2026). But satisfaction is an average, and averages hide the holiday-weekend outliers where manners matter most.

## The 80% rule, and why it exists

The single most useful thing any EV driver can know about rapid charging is that the last fifth of the battery is the slowest. Charging speeds taper sharply above about 80% state of charge, as the battery's management system deliberately slows the flow to protect the cells. The top-up from 80% to 100% can occupy the charger for nearly as long as the journey from 20% to 80% did, for a fraction of the range gained.

This is why experienced drivers treat 80% as the natural stopping point on a rapid charger. The habit is not self-denial; it is maths. Two stops of 20 minutes each, arriving at each charger low and leaving at 80%, will usually get you further down the motorway in less total time than one stop stretched to a full charge. The exception is the long gap between chargers on a remote route, where you genuinely need every mile, and the empty site at 2am, where nobody is waiting and courtesy has nothing to say.

At a busy site, holding a bay past 80% is the nearest thing the EV world has to a universal faux pas. If a queue is forming, unplug and go.

## The rest of the conventions

The 80% rule is the headline, but the supporting cast matters too.

Move when you are finished. A charging bay is not a parking space. Overstay fees have spread across the networks precisely because a completed car left plugged in blocks the bay for someone who needs it, and the fees work: the era of cars squatting on chargers for hours has largely faded where they are enforced. Most cars and apps will notify you when the session ends, so respond to the notification rather than finishing your shopping.

Queue fairly and visibly. The norm in the UK is informal first-come, first-served. At sites with multiple bays, park behind the line of cars that arrived before you rather than hovering by a specific stall. If you are charging and see someone waiting, that is the moment to check whether you already have enough charge to reach your destination. Most drivers do exactly this; the system mostly runs on people being reasonable with each other.

Do not unplug someone else's car. Even when a car appears finished, you cannot know whether the owner has scheduled the session, is preconditioning the battery for the next leg, or has simply nipped to the toilet with every intention of returning. Unplugging another driver's vehicle is the fastest route to a heated conversation in a car park, and some cars lock the connector anyway.

Match the charger to the job. If a site mixes ultra-rapid and slower units and you only need a modest top-up to get home, the slower unit does the job just as well and leaves the fast bay for someone with a long journey and a low battery. Equally, do not occupy a rapid charger for an hour while doing the weekly shop: rapid chargers are for charging, not parking. We looked at what the motorway charging experience is actually like in [motorway service chargers](/motorway-service-chargers/).

Leave the place tidy. Coil the cable back onto the unit rather than dumping it on the ground, where it becomes a trip hazard and a damage risk. If you find a broken charger, report it in the network's app. Reporting feels futile and is not: networks prioritise fixes by reported fault volumes, and the next driver's twenty-minute detour is avoided by the thirty seconds you spent.

## Why any of this matters

The practical answer is that good behaviour shortens queues. A site where everyone charges to 80% and moves promptly serves materially more cars per hour than one where drivers top up to full and wander off. On a busy day, that difference compounds down the queue, and the person who benefits from the driver two cars ahead moving quickly might be you on the return trip.

The broader answer is that the public network is still young, and its reputation is being set now. Overall satisfaction with electric cars themselves runs at [88% in Zapmap's 2025 survey, with only 3% of EV drivers saying they want to switch back to petrol or diesel](https://www.zapmap.com/news/ev-charging-survey-2025-results). The weakest link in an otherwise strong ownership experience is the shared infrastructure, which is getting better fast, as we charted in [the state of the UK charging network](/uk-charging-network-state/), but which still depends on thousands of small, unwitnessed acts of consideration every day.

The comparison with the petrol forecourt is worth making one last time. Nobody thanks you for moving your car away from the pump; it is simply what one does. EV charging will get there, and quickly, because the incentives all point the same way. Until then, the rules are simple enough to fit on a sticky note: charge what you need, stop around 80% when it is busy, move when you are done, and leave the bay as you found it. That is the whole code, and it is working.
