Renault has confirmed UK pricing for the new Twingo E-Tech electric, and the headline figure is £14,745. That is the entry Evolution trim after the full £3,750 Electric Car Grant is applied at the point of sale, taking the £18,495 list price below £15,000 and level with the cheapest petrol car on sale in Britain, the Dacia Sandero at £14,765 on the road. Order books open in the first quarter of 2027, with first deliveries expected by March.

The grant eligibility was the missing piece. Renault had long promised the Twingo would start under £20,000, but the final figure depended on which band of the Electric Car Grant the Slovenia-built car qualified for. The government has now confirmed it sits in Band 1, the top tier worth £3,750, awarded on the carbon intensity of where the car and its battery are made. Renault said on 19 November that official pricing would be revealed on 20 November.

## The price ladder

All three UK trims carry the full £3,750 grant. The entry Evolution starts at £14,745, the mid Techno at £16,245, and the top Launch Edition at £17,645, according to the pricing confirmed today and reported by [Autocar](https://autocar.co.uk/car-news/new-cars/official-renault-twingo-starts-under-%C2%A315k-%C2%A33750-ecg-discount), [Auto Express](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/renault/twingo/370951/new-renault-twingo-price-confirmed-and-under-ps15k) and [CAR magazine](https://carmagazine.co.uk/car-news/industry-news/renault/twingo-price-2027). Without the grant, the Evolution's £18,495 list price sits close to rivals such as the Citroen e-C3 Urban Range, which costs £18,495 after its own £1,500 Band 2 grant against a £19,995 list.

The comparison that matters for buyers is against other cheap cars, electric or not. The Twingo's £14,745 undercuts the BYD Dolphin Surf Active at £18,650, which gets no government grant because it is built in China, and the Hyundai Inster from £22,995, which is also outside the scheme. The cheapest electric cars on sale remain the Dacia Spring from £11,990 and the Leapmotor T03 at £12,995, both sold with manufacturer-funded discounts rather than the government grant. But the Twingo is the cheapest grant-eligible EV with real brand backing, and it is £20 below the £14,765 on-the-road price of the petrol Sandero that has held the cheapest-new-car crown, as [Dacia's own pricing release](https://press.dacia.co.uk/releases/539) sets out.

## What the money buys

The Twingo is 3.79 metres long with five doors, two independently sliding rear seats and up to 360 litres of boot space. All versions run a 60kW motor and a 27.5kWh LFP battery, giving a claimed range of up to 163 miles on the WLTP cycle, with 50kW DC fast charging available. The Evolution trim includes a 10-inch infotainment screen, a seven-inch driver display, cruise control and lane keep assist. Techno adds 18-inch wheels, Google built-in, heated front seats and automatic climate control; the Launch Edition adds blind-spot monitoring, rear cross-traffic alert and hands-free parking.

The battery is lithium iron phosphate, the same chemistry as the Nissan Pixo and every BYD on sale here, and it is part of why the price is this low. Renault has said the LFP approach cuts battery production cost by around 20%. The trade-off is cold-weather sensitivity, which Renault counters with battery preconditioning when a charging stop is programmed into the route.

## Why the grant matters so much here

The Electric Car Grant only applies to cars listed at £37,000 or below and approved by the Office for Zero Emission Vehicles, and the band is set by manufacturing carbon scores rather than by the car itself. Renault's European-built models sit in Band 1; Chinese-built cars such as BYD and Leapmotor models are excluded regardless of price. That is why a £18,495-list Twingo lands below a £18,650 Dolphin Surf. We have covered the full eligible list and how the bands work in [our guide to the grant list](/grant-eligible-cars-list/), and the wider affordable-EV shelf, including the Spring, T03 and Pixo, in [our guide to the cheapest new EVs](/small-evs-guide/).

The timing is worth noting in the context of the ZEV mandate debate. Battery electric cars took a 32.0% share of the UK market in October, their highest of the year, and 503,805 have been registered so far in 2026, up 30.4% on a year earlier, according to the [SMMT's October release](https://smmt.co.uk/year-end-push-lifts-ev-sales-but-mandate-gap-set-to-widen). Year to date, though, that is a 26.6% share against the 33% the 2026 mandate asks for, and the SMMT forecasts 27.2% for the full year. A sub-£15,000 European-built EV with the full grant is exactly the kind of offer that keeps the curve rising, and it arrives as ministers weigh up the mandate review while the Climate Change Committee urges the targets to stay in place. For the used market, where three-to-five-year-old EVs are now selling in 24 days, a cheap new entry point also sets the floor under second-hand values.
