Zemo Partnership has launched a campaign to encourage EV drivers with home chargers to share them with neighbours who cannot plug in at home, arguing that Britain's private chargepoints are among the most underused pieces of energy infrastructure in the country. The initiative, backed by the AA, Uber, Corpay's Allstar brand, EVA England and Zapmap, comes as industry data puts the number of UK households without straightforward access to home charging at more than 9 million, and as October's Cost of Public EV Charging Review set out how much more those households pay for their miles.

## The idle capacity already on driveways

Zapmap estimates there were [around 1.4 million home chargepoints installed at UK homes with driveways at the end of 2025](https://zapmap.com/ev-stats/home-and-community-charging), considerably more than the public network, which [passed 126,000 connections this week](/uk-charger-count-passes-126000-rapid-30000/). Much of that private capacity is used only once or twice a week. Meanwhile, more than 2 million fully electric cars and vans are now on UK roads, and most drivers who can charge at home do most of their charging there.

There is no official register of home charge devices. Zapmap's estimate combines the government's EVHS grant installation statistics with SMMT car registration data and its own annual EV Charging Survey, so it is an estimate rather than a count, and it focuses on battery cars, excluding home chargers bought for the plug-in hybrids registered since March 2022.

## The households left out

Of the 28.4 million households in the UK at the end of 2023, [67.2% have access to a driveway or dedicated parking close to the house](https://zapmap.com/ev-stats/home-and-community-charging), according to Field Dynamics analysis cited by Zapmap. That leaves 32.8%, or around 9 million households, without driveway access, and Zapmap's data shows EV uptake is significantly lower among households without convenient access to home charging. The same households are the ones most exposed to public charging prices, the two-tier cost of driving electric that [we have covered before](/public-home-price-gap/), and the group the planning barrier has historically shut out of cheap overnight charging, as [our look at the driveway problem](/charging-without-a-driveway/) explained.

## How sharing works in practice

Several platforms now connect hosts with chargers to neighbours who need one. The two main established players are Co Charger, which had 6,700 hosts, and JustCharge, with just over 4,000, [according to figures from June 2026 on Zapmap's community charging page](https://zapmap.com/ev-stats/home-and-community-charging). Drivers book a slot, plug in and walk home while the car charges overnight. Hosts can usually set their own prices, and depending on the platform the pricing can include a minimum amount per kWh plus a parking charge, while the platforms themselves charge different fees and some charge a subscription.

On safety and reliability, one charger-sharing platform, a Zemo member, reports no damage and no disputes in more than 17,000 sessions over five years, and fewer than 3% of its hosts stop sharing within a year. A 2025 survey by one platform found that 92% of respondents said knowing a nearby shareable charger would be available had positively influenced their decision to go electric.

## What a host can earn

Based on figures from one charger-sharing platform, hosting a single regular private driver could earn around £200 a year after fees and electricity costs, while hosting three or four drivers could generate additional income of around £600 to more than £1,000 a year, depending on usage and pricing. Research by University College London suggests many hosts are also motivated by helping neighbours switch to an EV or support cleaner air locally, not just the money.

Ruth, who has shared her home charger with neighbours in Bristol since October 2021 and has hosted 180 charging sessions, charges 38p per kWh, a rate she chose to be fair to people charging overnight. "All we have to do is remember to leave it turned on and take our car off the drive. It's not complicated at all," she said. "In over five years she has had no disputes and no damage."

## Why the push is landing now

The government's Cost of Public Electric Vehicle Charging Review, led by Philip New and published on [28 October 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cost-of-public-electric-vehicle-charging-review), found average public charging prices have risen [69% in real terms for lower-powered AC chargers and 94% for higher-powered DC chargers since 2021](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae0b2b77cf9b2248904678d/cost-of-public-ev-charging-review.pdf), putting the average standard public tariff at 54p per kWh and rapid and ultra-rapid at 79p per kWh. Its cost-per-mile analysis puts a driver who can mostly charge at home at 8p to 12p per mile, against 17p to 20p per mile for a driver reliant on a mix of public chargers, and 24p on rapid charging alone, compared with 22p for petrol at September 2026 prices.

Ministers have already moved on the related planning barrier: from [21 October 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/drivers-to-save-up-to-300-when-installing-ev-chargers-at-home-as-government-slashes-unnecessary-red-tape) drivers in England no longer need planning permission for qualifying cross-pavement chargers, saving up to £300 on installation and opening home-rate charging, which the government puts at as little as 2p per mile. Highway authority authorisation is still required, and conservation areas and listed buildings are excluded, as [our coverage of the rule change](/gully-red-tape-cut/) sets out. Charger sharing sits alongside that fix rather than replacing it, offering a route for households where a pavement channel is not possible at all.

Jonathan Murray, managing director of Zemo Partnership, said: "If we are serious about making EV ownership accessible to more people, we need to make better use of the charging capacity we already have." He added that sharing existing home chargepoints "is a simple, low-cost approach that can make local charging more accessible while giving hosts a worthwhile return. It can complement the continued roll-out of public charging, rather than replace it."

Edmund King OBE, AA president, said: "Charger sharing is a simple but clever way of making better use of current infrastructure." Vicky Edmonds, chief executive of EVA England, said: "Our research shows half of petrol and diesel drivers are holding back on buying an EV because of issues like the cost of public charging." Anshul Vohra, UK Electrification Lead at Uber, said: "We're happy to support this initiative, which gives drivers on the Uber platform a practical way to charge close to where they live."

## What sharing does not replace

Public charging continues to expand and will remain essential, particularly for longer journeys, and the campaign is explicitly positioned as complementary to that roll-out. Drivers still need reliable on-street and rapid provision for trips away from home, and hosts choose which bookings to accept, so availability is not guaranteed. But for the overnight top-up that makes up most of an EV's weekly energy, a neighbour's driveway charger at a rate like Ruth's 38p per kWh can undercut many public tariffs while paying the host for hardware that would otherwise sit idle.
