Budgets are easy to announce and hard to audit. Last November, the Chancellor stood up and set out an EV package that touched almost every part of electric car ownership: more grant money stretching to 2030, more chargers, an easier route to home charging for people without driveways, and a new pay-per-mile tax arriving in 2028. Five months on, it is worth separating what has actually landed from what is still a line in a speech. Here is the ledger.

## The grant: delivered, and growing

The biggest promise was money. The Electric Car Grant launched in July 2025 with a £650m fund, and the Budget [added £1.3bn on top, taking the scheme to £2bn and extending it to 2030](https://edie.net/budget-chancellor-confirms-pay-per-mile-ev-tax-and-funding-boost-for-chargers). That commitment is now the backbone of the affordable EV market. The grant pays £3,750 or £1,500 depending on the band, and the eligible list has grown past 35 models since the Budget, with manufacturers visibly designing their pricing around it: Renault now quotes UK prices for the updated R5 with the grant already deducted.

There is an honest debate about how well the money works, which we have covered before in [our analysis of whether the grant actually expands the market](/does-the-grant-actually-work/). Research from New AutoMotive, published before the Budget, found [EVs covered by the scheme made up 23.8% of new registrations, the same share as before the grant was announced](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c39pk873wlwo), suggesting it was mostly subsidising sales that would have happened anyway. The counter-argument is that a grant shelf full of sub-£30,000 cars changes what manufacturers choose to sell in Britain, and the growing list of eligible small EVs lends that view some support. Either way, the promise was made and the money is being spent.

For the drivers the grant is meant to reach, the shape of the market has changed as much as the total. When the scheme launched, eligible cars were mostly family hatchbacks and crossovers. This autumn the list runs from the Renault 5 and the incoming Nissan Pixo up through the Kia EV2, and the discount is now quoted inside headline prices rather than claimed back afterwards. That shift matters more than it sounds: a grant that changes the number on the windscreen sticker reaches buyers a discount form never will.

## Chargers: announced, part-spent, with a consultation still owed

The Budget also earmarked [£200m for chargepoints](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c39pk873wlwo), aimed at speeding up the rollout, particularly on local streets. The network itself has kept growing underneath it: the UK passed 123,677 public chargers by the end of August, up from under 87,000 when the Budget was delivered, and more than a thousand rapid hubs now dot the map. At that pace the government's 300,000 target for 2030 still looks distant, at roughly 41% covered with barely a quarter of the decade left, which is exactly why the extra money was promised. What is harder to trace is the exact accounting of the Budget's £200m, since government has not published a line-by-line spend against the announcement. The direction is real; the ledger entry remains open.

The same goes for the third charging promise. The Budget [committed to publish a consultation on Permitted Development Rights](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c39pk873wlwo), the planning rules that decide whether households can install a cross-pavement charging channel without a full planning application. For the many households without a driveway, this is the difference between an affordable installation and one that never gets approved. The consultation was promised in the Budget statement but had not been published by the time of writing, so this one sits in the "promised, not delivered" column. It matters most for the renters and flat owners the chargepoint grant was extended to cover, and we set out the practical options in [our guide to charging without a driveway](/charging-without-a-driveway/).

## The eVED clock: the promise nobody's waiting for

The least popular Budget promise was the pay-per-mile tax, and it is the one with a fixed deadline. From April 2028, electric car drivers will pay [3p per mile, and plug-in hybrid drivers 1.5p per mile, with rates rising with inflation](https://edie.net/budget-chancellor-confirms-pay-per-mile-ev-tax-and-funding-boost-for-chargers). The government forecasts the typical EV driver will pay around £240 a year, and the Office for Budget Responsibility estimates the tax will [raise about £1.1bn in 2028-29](https://zagdaily.com/micromobility/uk-autumn-budget-announces-new-mileage-tax-for-electric-vehicles).

Nothing about that has shifted since November, which is the point: the tax is legislated for and the clock is running. A consultation on how the eVED system works in practice is under way, covering mileage verification and privacy, with the government promising the system will not track where or when journeys are taken. The RAC Foundation has warned the impact assessment suggests 120,000 fewer EVs sold over the next five years than would otherwise be the case, a warning that sits awkwardly beside the government's own target of 80% zero-emission new car sales by 2030. How seriously to take it is genuinely uncertain: the tax works out at roughly £240 a year for a typical driver, which is real money but a fraction of what an equivalent petrol driver pays in fuel duty. The risk is less the size of the charge than the signal it sends at the moment the market is finally reaching price parity. For a driver buying an electric car this autumn, the practical summary is simple: the first-year running costs are unchanged, and a few pence per mile arrives in 2028. We have set out the detail in [our guide to the 3p per mile question](/the-3p-per-mile-question/).

## What to watch next

Two dates matter. The ZEV mandate review consultation, launched in August, [closes on 23 October 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/review-launched-to-shape-pathway-to-reach-zero-emission-driving-by-2035), and its outcome will shape the market the grant operates in. And the grant list keeps moving, which we track in [our autumn update on the Electric Car Grant](/grants-autumn-update/).

The fair verdict on the Budget's EV package is: the grant promise fully landed, the charger money is flowing, the planning consultation is late, and the tax is coming exactly as scheduled. That is a more honest scorecard than either the cheerleaders or the critics tend to offer.
