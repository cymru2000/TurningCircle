New research published ahead of the government's pending decision on the ZEV mandate puts a figure on the gap between small businesses that can charge vans at their own premises and those that cannot: £150,000 over five years for a modelled 10-van construction firm. The study, by the Centre for Economics and Business Research (Cebr) for the Open Road Partnership, also records that battery electric vans took just [11.0% of new van registrations in the year to October 2026](https://vanfleetworld.co.uk/smes-face-150k-electric-van-cost-divide-research-warns/), less than half the 24% the mandate requires of manufacturers this year.

## What the research found

Cebr modelled three small-business archetypes: a construction company running 10 vans, a surveying business with five company cars and a sole-trader window cleaner. For the 10-van firm, installing private charging changes the entire economics of the switch. [The research finds the business would save around £42,000 over five years compared with running diesel vans](https://greenfleet.net/news/19112026/government-urged-give-smes-fairer-route-electric), even after absorbing roughly [£15,500 of additional costs in the year of purchase](https://vanfleetworld.co.uk/smes-face-150k-electric-van-cost-divide-research-warns/), driven mainly by the upfront price of vehicles and charging hardware. Annual costs fall below diesel from 2027, and the business breaks even by 2028.

Relying on public chargers tells the opposite story. The same 10-van fleet would be [£110,000 worse off over five years](https://greenfleet.net/news/19112026/government-urged-give-smes-fairer-route-electric) once lost working time is counted, because vehicles and employees spend part of the working day travelling to and waiting at public charge points. That lost productive output alone is worth [more than £23,800 a year](https://vanfleetworld.co.uk/smes-face-150k-electric-van-cost-divide-research-warns/). The total cost gap between the private-charging and public-charging scenarios reaches [£150,000 over the five years](https://businessmotoring.co.uk/smes-face-150000-cost-gap-in-electric-van-transition-research-finds/).

Financing does not solve the problem for the smallest operators. The modelled sole trader who finances an electric van instead of buying a diesel van outright ends up around [£100 worse off after five years](https://vanfleetworld.co.uk/smes-face-150k-electric-van-cost-divide-research-warns/), because the finance charges eat the savings the switch would otherwise deliver.

## Why charging access decides the maths

Vans are workplaces, so every minute spent finding or waiting at a public charger is time the van is not earning. Public rapid charging also costs far more per kWh than a depot or home tariff, and the price gap widens with every mile driven. For a tradesperson on a fixed daily round, a public charging stop can push the total cost of running an electric van above the equivalent diesel figure, which is exactly what the Cebr modelling shows.

Depot charging is the lever that flips the maths, and it is where government support matters most. The Department for Transport's Depot Charging Scheme is meant to part-fund chargers at fleet premises, but its [£38m second round, due to open on 28 October, has still not opened for applications](/depot-charging-window-misses-opening-date/). For small firms weighing a switch, that delay sits directly on the wrong side of the cost divide the research describes.

## The mandate gap behind the numbers

The 11.0% year-to-date share sits against a [24% zero-emission target for vans in 2026 under the Vehicle Emissions Trading Schemes Order 2023](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/vehicle-emissions-trading-schemes-vets-order-2023-updates/updates-to-the-vehicle-emissions-trading-schemes-vets-order-2023), rising to [70% by 2030](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2023/1394/schedule/6/part/2). October itself was strong: [3,369 battery electric vans were registered, up 58.0% on the year and a 12.9% share of a van market that grew 11.4% to 25,510](https://www.smmt.co.uk/year-end-push-lifts-ev-sales-but-mandate-gap-set-to-widen), according to the SMMT's 5 November release. That monthly pace is still well short of the mandate, as [our earlier analysis of the October figures](/electric-van-record-mandate-gap-doubles/) set out.

The government's [review of the mandate, launched on 14 August 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/review-launched-to-shape-pathway-to-reach-zero-emission-driving-by-2035), closed its consultation on 23 October, and a decision on the targets is now expected imminently. The Cebr work was commissioned specifically to inform that decision.

## What the partnership is asking for

The Open Road Partnership, which brings together United Rental Group, Zenith, Enterprise Mobility and Vertu Motors, is urging ministers to use the mandate review to set a realistic trajectory for electric car and van sales, backed by stronger demand incentives. It also wants faster access to affordable public charging, the removal of barriers to installing depot infrastructure, and more flexibility for small businesses to electrify in line with their replacement cycles.

Per Voegerl, chief executive of United Rental Group, said: "Small and medium-sized businesses are ready to play their part in the EV transition, but for many, the economics of making the switch simply don't stack up yet. The challenge isn't whether to electrify, but how quickly businesses can realistically make the transition."

## What it means for tradespeople and small fleets

The research describes a divide, not a refusal. Demand for electric vans is growing, and [used electric van values have been rising even as diesel van values fall](/used-electric-van-values-rise/), which shows buyers exist at the right price. The obstacle for the smallest firms is the combination of upfront cost and charging access, and that is a solvable infrastructure question rather than a demand question. If the mandate targets are adjusted in the coming weeks, the £150,000 gap between a depot charger and a public one will still decide whether electrification pays back for a sole trader. Whatever trajectory ministers set, depot and workplace charging access is the difference between the two outcomes the research prices out.
