The average licensed car in the UK was 10.64 years old at the end of December 2025, according to the RAC Foundation's reading of the Department for Transport's vehicle licensing statistics. That is the oldest the fleet has ever been, and it is roughly 30 per cent older than at the end of 2020. On the SMMT's separate measure of vehicles actually in use, the average car was 9.7 years old in 2025, up from 9.5 in 2024, with a record 45.7 per cent of cars now more than a decade old.

An ageing fleet is not a curiosity. It decides what is on forecourts, what your next MOT bill looks like, and how quickly the switch to electric can actually happen.

## Two measures, one direction

The two headline figures come from different datasets and both are correct. The DfT's [vehicle licensing statistics for 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/vehicle-licensing-statistics-2025/vehicle-licensing-statistics-united-kingdom-2025), published on 29 April 2026, count every licensed vehicle in the UK at the end of December 2025: 42.3 million in total, up 1 per cent on 2024, with an average car age rounded to 10 years in its summary table. The RAC Foundation's more granular calculation of the same DVLA data puts the average at 10.64 years, and records 2,012,000 licensed zero emission vehicles, up 31.2 per cent on the year before.

The SMMT's [Motorparc data](https://smmt.co.uk/one-in-22-vehicles-now-zero-emission-as-uk-fleet-reaches-record-high), published on 24 April 2026, counts the fleet in use rather than everything on the register. It put the total parc at a record 42,549,649 vehicles in 2025, up 1.4 per cent, with cars rising by more than half a million to 36,676,185. The gap between 9.7 and 10.64 is mostly vehicles sitting with a SORN, but both series point the same way: British motorists are holding onto cars longer than ever.

## The age gap between petrol and electric

The average hides the real story, which is the spread by fuel type. At the end of 2025, the average petrol car was 11.07 years old and the average diesel 10.85, while the average battery electric car was just 3.51 years old and the average plug-in hybrid 4.39, per the [RAC Foundation's motoring facts and figures](https://www.racfoundation.org/motoring-faqs/mobility). The gap between a petrol car and an electric one is therefore about 7.5 years, and it is almost entirely a story of when each technology arrived in volume: petrol and diesel cars have been on sale for a century, while 528,000 zero emission vehicles were registered for the first time in 2025 alone, up 24 per cent, according to the [DfT 2025 statistics](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/vehicle-licensing-statistics-2025/vehicle-licensing-statistics-united-kingdom-2025).

That split matters for the used market. A used EV buyer is almost always buying a young car with a young battery, while a used petrol buyer at the average age is buying something over 11 years old. Our guide to [why 100,000 miles on a used EV is no longer a red flag](/used-ev-reliability-study/) looks at what the MOT data says about high-mileage electric cars, and the answer is more reassuring than many expect.

## A quarter of cars are over 13

The fleet is not just older on average, it is heavier at the old end. In 2025, 25.1 per cent of the UK car fleet was over 13 years old, up from 23.6 per cent in 2023 and 13.3 per cent in 2014, according to the [RAC Foundation FAQ](https://www.racfoundation.org/motoring-faqs/mobility). Applied to the 34.89 million cars licensed at the end of June 2026, that is roughly 8.7 million cars now 13 or older.

Why so many? New registrations tell part of it. Only 2,617,000 vehicles were registered for the first time in the UK in 2025, an increase of less than 1 per cent on 2024, per the [DfT statistics](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/vehicle-licensing-statistics-2025/vehicle-licensing-statistics-united-kingdom-2025). The other part is the used market, where buyers keep older cars circulating. The UK's used car market grew 2.2 per cent in 2025 to [7,807,872 transactions](https://www.smmt.co.uk/evs-power-up-used-car-market-in-three-year-growth-run/), the third consecutive year of growth, according to the SMMT's figures published on 10 February 2026. Petrol and diesel still made up 89.9 per cent of those transactions, with petrol alone at 56.7 per cent.

## What it means for buyers

For anyone shopping used, the average age of the parc is the average age of what is actually for sale. Cars and taxis travelled 262.7 billion vehicle miles in the 12 months to June 2026, up 1.6 per cent on the year before, per the [RAC Foundation](https://www.racfoundation.org/motoring-faqs/mobility), and most of those miles are being driven by cars older than a decade. Our look at [how far UK drivers actually drive](/how-far-do-uk-drivers-drive/) shows how few of those miles most journeys need, which is why an older car can still be a rational buy if its history is sound.

The practical check is the same whatever the fuel: a full service history, a recent MOT, and honest mileage. A car over 13 is not automatically finished, but the odds of a big bill rise with every year. Dealers' own answer to the ageing fleet has been a scrappage scheme, with the most popular design in a survey of 60 retailers paying £4,000 against a trade-in more than 10 years old, as we covered in [the scrappage survey story](/ev-scrappage-scheme-dealers-survey/).

## What it means for the EV switch

An old petrol fleet is also the opportunity for the EV transition. One in 22 vehicles on UK roads is now zero emission, per the [SMMT's Motorparc release](https://smmt.co.uk/one-in-22-vehicles-now-zero-emission-as-uk-fleet-reaches-record-high), and used BEV sales surged 45.7 per cent in 2025 to a record 274,815 transactions, a 3.5 per cent share of the used market, up from 2.5 per cent in 2024, according to the [SMMT's used car figures](https://www.smmt.co.uk/evs-power-up-used-car-market-in-three-year-growth-run/). Electrified cars of all kinds took 9.9 per cent of used sales.

But 89.9 per cent of used transactions were still petrol or diesel, and the used EV supply, while growing fast, is young and thin at the cheap end. That is why the age profile cuts both ways: the same cost pressures that keep the average car at over 10 years also make a sub-£10,000 used EV hard to find. Our [used EV range and value tracker](/used-ev-range-sweet-spot/) follows where that supply is thickening, and the [public charger count milestone](/uk-charger-count-passes-126000-rapid-30000/) shows the charging side keeping pace with a 2.33 million strong electric parc.
