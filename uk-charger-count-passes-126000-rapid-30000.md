The UK's public charging network passed two round-number milestones in the same month. Zapmap's end-of-October figures, the dataset that underpins the Department for Transport's official charging statistics, put the national total at [126,140 public chargers across 48,815 locations](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/ev-charging-statistics), and the count of rapid and ultra-rapid units at 30,060. For a country that was still fitting its first motorway rapid chargers a decade ago, the scale of the network has become hard to argue with.

## The October count

At the end of October 2026 the UK had 126,140 public EV chargers sitting on 98,899 physical devices, according to [Zapmap's monthly statistics](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/ev-charging-statistics), updated on 4 November. The network added a net 1,402 chargers during the month, taking the year-to-date total of new chargers to 10,088 and the year-on-year growth rate to 9.6%. The Department for Transport published its own faster indicator for 1 November 2026 on 11 November, drawing on the same Zapmap data, which keeps the official series and the industry count in step ([GOV.UK charging infrastructure statistics](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/electric-vehicle-charging-infrastructure-statistics)).

One detail in the methodology is worth knowing before quoting the total. Since the start of 2026 the headline metric has been EV chargers, individual sockets, rather than devices, so a twin-socket pedestal now counts as two. That makes the 126,140 figure directly comparable with the DfT's official series but not with pre-2026 device counts.

## Rapid and ultra-rapid pass 30,000

The faster end of the network crossed its own milestone. There were [30,060 rapid (50kW to 149kW) and ultra-rapid (150kW and above) chargers at the end of October](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/ev-charging-statistics), spread across 7,202 locations, with 338 net new units added during the month and year-on-year growth of 15%. The split between the two power bands has now flipped: 15,608 ultra-rapid chargers against 14,452 rapid ones, meaning Britain has more chargers capable of 150kW or more than it has in the entire 50kW to 149kW category.

By use case, the same figures break down as 14,225 en-route chargers, 64,903 at destination locations and 41,927 on-street. The en-route share is small by count but carries the long-journey load, and it is where the ultra-rapid growth is concentrated.

## Hubs pass 1,091

The number of charging hubs, locations with at least eight rapid or ultra-rapid chargers, reached 1,091 at the end of October, up from 926 at the end of 2025 and 723 at the end of 2024 ([Zapmap](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/ev-charging-statistics)). The site passed the 1,000-hub mark earlier this autumn; our [September look at that milestone](/rapid-hubs-1000/) covered what the hub era changes for electric journeys. A hub count of 1,091 means long-distance routes are now served by banked high-power sites rather than lone rapid posts, which is the single biggest practical change for drivers covering motorway distances.

## One charger for every 18 electric cars

The fleet side of the ratio matters as much as the charger side. [Zapmap's EV market page](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/ev-market) puts fully electric cars on UK roads at over 2,330,000 at the end of October 2026, around 6.7% of the roughly 34.8 million cars in the parc. Dividing that fleet by the 126,140 public chargers gives about one public charger for every 18 battery electric cars. The SMMT's registration tables show the flow behind the stock: [503,805 battery electric cars were registered in the first ten months of 2026, up 30.4% on the same period last year](https://www.smmt.co.uk/vehicle-data/car-registrations/), the figure behind October's record 32.0% monthly share covered in [our 5 November piece](/electric-cars-32-percent-october/).

The DfT's quarterly licensing statistics, published on 23 September for the period to June, put licensed zero emission vehicles at 2.313 million, up 30.7% year on year and 5.4% of all licensed vehicles, with 2,017,000 of them cars ([GOV.UK vehicle licensing statistics, April to June 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/vehicle-licensing-statistics-april-to-june-2026)). The gap between those two measures, new sales against the whole licensed fleet, is why the on-road share will keep lagging the showroom share for years.

The pace comparison is the uncomfortable part. Our [11 November piece on charger additions](/public-charger-additions-fall-18-percent-2026/) noted that the 10,088 chargers added in the first ten months of 2026 run 18% behind the same period of 2025 while electric registrations rose 30.4%. The network is still growing, but the fleet is growing faster.

## What the milestones mean at the kerb

For drivers, the headline counts translate into a different question than the one a decade ago: not whether a charger exists, but whether it is fast, working and priced sanely. Zapmap's October price index put the weighted average pay-as-you-go price at 54p/kWh on standard and standard-plus chargers and 82p/kWh on rapid and ultra-rapid units ([Zapmap Charging Price Index](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/charging-price-index)). Rapid and ultra-rapid units are only about 24% of chargers by count, and they carry the journeys where waiting time costs the most. The grid-charge pressures behind those prices, and the forecast of £1 per kWh average rapid pricing in 2027, are set out in [our 12 November analysis](/rapid-charging-1-per-kwh-grid-charges/).

The milestones are real, and they are cumulative: 126,140 chargers, 30,060 of them rapid or better, 1,091 hubs, against a fleet of 2.33 million electric cars and rising at around 50,000 registrations a month. The open question the October data leaves is whether the installation pace, running 18% behind last year, can keep up with a fleet that is not slowing down.
