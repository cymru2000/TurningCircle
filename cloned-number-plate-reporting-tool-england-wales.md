Drivers in England and Wales who suspect their number plate has been cloned can now report it through a single online form, after police and the DVLA launched a joint national service this month. The tool, delivered by the National Police Chiefs' Council through the Single Online Home, sends the report to the local police force and shares it automatically with the DVLA, replacing the old process of contacting each separately.

The launch lands against record figures for the crime. The DVLA was contacted by 11,402 drivers in 2025 over fines or penalties they did not recognise or accept responsibility for, according to [an RAC analysis of DVLA data published on 16 October](https://rac.co.uk/drive/news/motoring-news/cloned-vehicles-rise-to-record-high). That is a 9% rise on the 10,460 contacts recorded in 2024 and 93% above the 5,912 recorded in 2018, the RAC said. The DVLA cautions that the total also covers ANPR misreads and administrative errors, so not every contact is a confirmed clone.

## How the new service works

Victims report through the cloned plate form on their local police force website, reachable via the Report tab on the Single Online Home. The form gives a bespoke route, issues a receipt code, and passes the report to the DVLA so it is logged against the vehicle's record. Reports can be made on behalf of the registered keeper, for example by a family member or a fleet operator, with the keeper's permission. The service is available in England and Wales only; in Scotland and Northern Ireland victims still call 101 and notify the DVLA separately, as [GOV.UK's guidance sets out](https://www.gov.uk/report-cloned-number-plate).

Crucially, the tool does not cancel fines. Victims must still contact the organisation that issued each penalty, penalty notice or parking charge and ask it to review the evidence, quoting the police report reference and the DVLA confirmation letter. Neither the police nor the DVLA can contest charges on a victim's behalf. Drivers can keep driving their own car after a clone report, though police may stop it to check the report reference, the V5C log book and a driving licence.

## What the record figures show

The Guardian's [22 November report on the new tool](https://www.theguardian.com/money/2026/nov/22/cloned-number-plate-victims-england-and-wales-new-reporting-tool) put the scale of the problem in human terms: Sandra Yarwood, a Londoner whose black Volkswagen Golf did not leave her home while she was in Canada, received 32 fines totalling £2,560 across five London boroughs after a similar car carrying a copy of her plate was caught on camera. She said contesting them was "gobsmacking" because "you have to prove your innocence and the fact that I was not in the country was not enough". It took her months to clear the file.

The RAC's head of policy, Simon Williams, said the figures confirmed that cloning had become "the menace of modern-day motoring", leaving innocent drivers to prove they were not driving at the time and place camera evidence shows. He argued that fraudsters pick a similar make, model and colour so the clone does not attract attention, and that it is only when a fine arrives that the real keeper finds out. An investigation last year found at least 130 official number plate suppliers willing to sell cloned versions, the Guardian reported.

## Why enforcement gaps make it worse

Cloning sits alongside the separate problem of "ghost" plates, which are designed to be unreadable by the UK's 18,000 ANPR cameras and are used to evade speed cameras, bus lanes and clean air zones. The RAC says both problems point to the same fix: suppliers carrying out the V5C log book and identity checks they are supposed to, and higher security standards for plates. The government's response has been building since September, when it announced a [government-funded roads policing centre](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/crackdown-on-illegal-number-plates-with-new-government-funded-roads-policing-team) to co-ordinate forces against illegal plates, and early police findings this year also showed that a third of motorists stopped in number plate operations did not know their own plate was non-compliant, which is why consumer guidance now sits alongside the reporting tool.

The enforcement backdrop has shifted under the issue. As [our coverage of the smart motorway camera revocation explained](/smart-motorway-speed-cameras-type-approval-revoked/), 154 variable speed cameras lost their legal approval on 17 November, and the DVSA's 2026/27 business plan promises a fraud lab to tackle false plates, so the cameras that would catch a cloned plate are themselves under review.

## What to do if your plate is cloned

The practical route, per [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/report-cloned-number-plate), is to report to police first using the online form in England and Wales, keep the reference number, then tell the DVLA by post if the form was not used, and appeal each fine with the issuer using the confirmation letters as evidence. When buying a used car, [the government's checks](https://www.gov.uk/checks-when-buying-a-used-car) advise confirming the registration number matches the details held by the DVLA, which is the cheapest protection against inheriting a cloned plate, and our [guide to buying a used EV](/before-you-buy-used-ev/) covers the wider checks worth making on any second-hand purchase.
