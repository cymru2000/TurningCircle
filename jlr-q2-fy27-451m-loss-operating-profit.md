JLR has reported a [£451m loss for the three months to 30 September](https://media.jlr.com/corporate/news/2026/11/jlr-delivers-operating-profit-second-quarter-and-launches-three-luxury-electric), its second quarter of the FY27 financial year, but the company says it delivered positive operational profit once the exceptional items are set aside. Revenue rose 20.3% to £5.9bn as recovery from last year's cyber attack flowed through, and the loss itself is dominated by £530m of restructuring charges linked to its job cuts. The same release confirms three new electric vehicles built in Solihull, with deliveries from early 2027.

The quarter sits between two stories we have already covered: the [volume recovery that took UK wholesales up sharply](/jlr-quarter-recovery/) and the [restructure programme targeting around 4,000 roles over two years](/jlr-restructure-2026/). What is new in these results is the money. The operational line turned positive for both the quarter and the half year, even as the headline loss stayed large enough to swallow it.

## A profit that exists only on one line

The cleanest way to read the result is the split between two lines. [Profit before tax and exceptional items was £6m in the quarter](https://media.jlr.com/corporate/news/2026/11/jlr-delivers-operating-profit-second-quarter-and-launches-three-luxury-electric), up from a loss of £485m a year earlier, which is the figure JLR uses to say it made an operating profit. The adjusted EBIT margin for the quarter was 1.1%, up from -8.6% a year ago. For the first half, profit before tax and exceptional items was £115m against a loss of £134m, with H1 revenue of £11.9bn up 3.2%.

That is genuine progress for a company that last year saw its factories taken offline by a cyber attack. But it is also thin. A 1.1% margin on £5.9bn of revenue is roughly £65m, which means the business is still running close to its break-even point while spending heavily to get there. The release attributes the year-on-year improvement to volumes, to the non-recurrence of the cyber-related costs from a year ago, and to a reduction in VME and structural costs, so part of the gain is simply last year's disaster falling out of the comparison.

## Where the £451m comes from

The gap between that positive operating line and the [£451m loss after tax](https://media.jlr.com/corporate/news/2026/11/jlr-delivers-operating-profit-second-quarter-and-launches-three-luxury-electric) is the exceptional items. JLR recorded £530m of them in the quarter, which it describes as covering restructuring activities including separation costs, a write down of intangible assets under development and a write down of assets. A year earlier the comparable loss was £559m, so the current result is an improvement on paper even though the underlying business is still not comfortably profitable.

The cash picture is less comfortable. Free cash outflow for the quarter was £540m, with a closing cash balance of £1.8bn. Total liquidity stood at £6.0bn as at 30 September, which JLR says includes an undrawn £1.7bn revolving credit facility, an undrawn £1.5bn UKEF guaranteed commercial loan and a new £1.0bn syndicated term loan signed on 29 September. That is a healthy buffer, but it also signals how much the company is leaning on its balance sheet to fund the transition.

## The job cuts now have a number attached

The restructuring is no longer just an announcement. [JLR said it expects the vast majority of the around 4,000 role reductions to come through voluntary redundancies, with 3,000 already confirmed](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/nov/13/jaguar-land-rover-quarterly-loss-electric-cars-job-cuts), and most of those people are expected to leave by the end of November. That leaves roughly 1,000 roles still to be confirmed out of the total, which is why the £530m charge was booked this quarter: the cuts were announced near the end of the period but the costs landed inside it.

The programme targets the salaried and management workforce rather than the production lines, as we set out when the plan was first detailed. The savings target remains about £1.7bn over the next two years, with the aim of reducing the number of vehicles JLR needs to sell to reach cash break-even towards 300,000 a year. For a company that sold well under that level in recent years, that is the single most consequential number in the results, because it defines how much volume the business actually has to move before it stops losing money.

## China pressure and a call for UK-EU co-operation

The other recurring theme in these results is competition from China. JLR's [wholesale volumes in China fell 45% year on year in the quarter, to 6,100 units from 11,400 a year ago](https://media.jlr.com/corporate/news/2026/10/jlr-delivers-growth-uk-and-overseas-markets-offsetting-challenges-china-and-middle), and the company said the Chinese market itself was [down almost 30%](https://www.businessday.co.za/motoring/2026-11-13-jlr-aims-for-double-digit-earnings-growth-in-major-restructure/) in the period, so falling volumes there, together with weakness in the Middle East, weighed on performance even as stronger wholesale in the UK and other overseas markets helped offset some of the decline. The release notes that Chinese manufacturers have become increasingly competitive, particularly in electric vehicles, where established carmakers face pressure on pricing, technology and product development.

Chief executive PB Balaji used the results to widen the argument beyond JLR. In comments reported by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/nov/13/jaguar-land-rover-quarterly-loss-electric-cars-job-cuts), he said the "onslaught of competition" from China meant the UK and EU should stop plans for new post-Brexit trade barriers on each other, adding that it is "so important for us that we all hold hands, stick together and ensure that we get the system to evolve into the world of electrics." The industry is lobbying to delay the implementation of rules of origin due from 1 January, which would risk a 10% tariff on UK-EU electric cars that use batteries sourced from Asia, so this is not just corporate commentary but part of a broader trade fight.

## Revised guidance and what comes next

The company cut its full-year outlook in the same breath. Guidance for the year has been revised to an adjusted EBIT margin in the range of 2-3%, down from around 4% previously, with free cash outflow expected at circa £1.25bn over the year. The release attributes that to market challenges, geopolitical risks and the exceptional costs recognised in the quarter, so the guidance cut is partly a one-off charge working through the numbers and partly a more cautious read on demand.

The products are where JLR wants the story to go next. It launched three new luxury electric vehicles this quarter, the Range Rover Electric, the Range Rover Sport Electric and the Jaguar Type 01, all built in Solihull with deliveries from early 2027, and has a Range Rover GT and a new electric Defender due next year, both to be built in Halewood on its new EMA architecture. Those vehicles sit inside a planned £15-18bn investment programme of which £11.5bn has been invested since 2024. Whether the revenue growth seen this quarter can turn into sustained margin, and whether the break-even point comes down fast enough, is what the coming quarters will test.
