The Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency (DVSA) has published its business plan for 2026 to 2027, setting out a review of MOT checks for advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS) and electric vehicles, a target of [2.3 million car practical driving tests by March 2027](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dvsa-business-plan-2026-to-2027/driver-and-vehicle-standards-agency-business-plan-2026-to-2027), and a commitment to bring the national average test waiting time down to [seven weeks or fewer by autumn 2027](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dvsa-business-plan-2026-to-2027/driver-and-vehicle-standards-agency-business-plan-2026-to-2027). The plan appeared on GOV.UK on 16 November and was announced in Parliament the same day in a written statement by the Minister of State for Transport.

For garages and drivers, the most consequential passages concern the MOT itself. The agency will carry out extended ADAS testing across both MOT and enforcement settings, covering light and heavy vehicles, and will review EV-specific test content, including the safe testing of high-voltage systems and battery integrity. The plan is explicit that this is development work: it does not yet confirm when, or whether, any additional checks become mandatory.

## What the plan says about driver aids in the MOT

Under its work on modern, technology-ready MOT and heavy vehicle tests, the DVSA says it will undertake extended testing of ADAS across both MOT and enforcement, determining what should be tested, how tests should be conducted safely and consistently, and what sanctions should apply for noncompliance. The same workstream covers roadside inspections and targeted enforcement activity, with proportionate sanctions where issues are found.

The stated aim is to keep roadworthiness frameworks fit for modern and increasingly automated vehicles, and the agency points to its role in the Automated Vehicle Act implementation programme. Nothing in the plan changes what a tester must check this month; the output of the review will shape future standards.

## EV batteries and high-voltage systems

Electric vehicles are named as a priority in the same section. The DVSA will review and evolve EV-specific test content across both MOT and commercial vehicle testing, including the safe testing of high-voltage systems and battery integrity. The work aligns with the wider push on connected and automated mobility, so that regulatory and safety frameworks keep pace with software-enabled vehicles.

The agency will also support HM Treasury's work on electric vehicle excise duty, examining its relationship with the MOT and collaborating with the DVLA. That sits alongside the per-mile charge for electric cars confirmed for April 2028, which our [guide to EV road tax](/ved-ev-owners-guide/) sets out in full. The plan does not link the two policies directly, but the mention signals that MOT records and vehicle data are expected to play a role in how mileage-based charging is administered.

## Driving tests: 2.3 million and a seven-week wait

Reducing car practical driving test waiting times is described as the agency's top operational priority. The plan records that [2.169 million tests were delivered in 2025 to 2026 against a target of 1.975 million](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dvsa-business-plan-2026-to-2027/driver-and-vehicle-standards-agency-business-plan-2026-to-2027), and commits to capacity of up to 2.3 million tests in 2026 to 2027. The seven-week average waiting time is targeted for autumn 2027, a date that falls after the plan period ends, with intermediate reductions to be delivered during the year.

Other waiting-time targets for the year to March 2027 are [theory tests at four weeks or under, vocational practical tests at three weeks or less, and motorcycle practical tests at six weeks or less](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dvsa-business-plan-2026-to-2027/driver-and-vehicle-standards-agency-business-plan-2026-to-2027). The agency plans to get there through increased testing capacity, an expanded examiner workforce, strengthened booking rules, improved scheduling and better use of data, alongside its Ready to Pass campaign.

## Fraud, enforcement and vehicle data

The plan sets a performance target of [28,000 risk-based, targeted actions to detect serious roadworthiness defects and traffic offences](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dvsa-business-plan-2026-to-2027/driver-and-vehicle-standards-agency-business-plan-2026-to-2027). It also commits to maintaining the number of MOT cases acted on for the most serious fraud, dishonesty and negligence, and to [increasing by 10% the number of cases laid to court from those investigations](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dvsa-business-plan-2026-to-2027/driver-and-vehicle-standards-agency-business-plan-2026-to-2027), measured from the 2024 to 2025 baseline.

A new fraud lab is promised, incorporating drone technology, forensic digital analysis, wireless router interrogation and new datamining techniques, alongside the national ANPR service for detecting driver hours fraud, speed limiter manipulation and false number plates. The agency will make greater use of vehicle-generated data to identify safety defects, roadworthiness issues and fraud, and will develop enforcement from the record so that decisions rest on multiple data sources rather than roadside checks alone.

## Fees, garages and the agency's own fleet

MOT, commercial vehicle testing, approvals and licensing fees are all flagged as an area for review, with the DVSA working with the Department for Transport and HM Treasury on charges that recover service costs. Changes to the pit fees charged by authorised testing facilities are to be implemented where appropriate, following the 2025 to 2026 consultation. Satisfaction targets for the year include 85% of MOT centres and authorised testing facilities satisfied with the service, and 80% of operators.

For context on how MOT rules evolve in practice, the agency's [special notice on LED replacement bulbs in halogen headlamps](/mot-led-bulb-rules/), which took effect in October, shows the notice route it uses when test content changes. Separately, the DVSA commits to reaching 100% zero-emission vehicles in its own fleet by December 2027, and it employs [around 5,000 colleagues](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/dvsa-business-plan-2026-to-2027/driver-and-vehicle-standards-agency-business-plan-2026-to-2027).

## What it means for drivers and garages

For the moment, nothing changes at the test centre. The practical effect of the plan over the next 16 months is more examiner capacity and shorter waits, a clearer idea of how driver-assistance systems might be checked in future, and firmer action against MOT fraud. Garages should watch for consultations on testing fees and pit fees, since those are the items most likely to alter operating costs. Drivers of electric cars should note that EV test content is being reviewed now, which matters as more high-mileage EVs reach their first MOTs.
