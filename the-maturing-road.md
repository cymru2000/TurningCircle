# Finding the Right Electric Car in 2026: What the Numbers Say and Which Models We Recommend

Choosing an electric car used to mean choosing between a handful of options. That is no longer true. The UK market now offers more than sixty fully electric models, prices have fallen across most segments, and the data tells us exactly what other drivers are doing with theirs. This guide sets out where the market stands right now, what the ownership numbers look like, and which cars we would actually recommend for different budgets and needs.

## Where the numbers stand

The industry body SMMT publishes registration data every month, and the year to date picture is unambiguous: [327,683 battery electric cars were registered in the UK between January and July 2026](https://www.smmt.co.uk/vehicle-data/electric-vehicle-registrations), up 28.7% on the same period last year. That gives BEVs a 25.3% share of all new car registrations, against 21.5% in July 2025. The full-year record was set in 2025, when [473,000 electric cars were sold](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/jan/06/uk-car-sales-2025-chinese-brands-electric-car-smmt), or 23.4% of a market that topped two million sales for the first time since 2019.

On the ownership side, [Zap-Map's statistics](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/ev-market) show there were 3,316,249 plug-in cars registered in the UK as of the end of July 2026, including more than 2.15 million fully electric ones. A figure worth pausing over: used EV sales hit a record 110,761 units in Q2 2026, up 67% on a year earlier. The second-hand market is no longer a discount bin for unwanted company cars. It has become a genuine entry point into electric motoring.

One more number from the SMMT data deserves attention, because it tells you something about pricing. Carmakers are offering an average of £11,000 in discounts on each electric car sold, against roughly £6,000 for petrol and diesel equivalents. That is a cumulative cost to the industry of £5.5 billion in 2025 alone. For buyers, it means list prices overstate what cars actually change hands for, and it means the gap between EV and combustion pricing has closed far more than headline figures suggest.

## The policy picture

Two policy items shape the buying decision right now. First, the [Electric Car Grant](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/four-more-ev-models-qualify-for-3750-discount-under-electric-car-grant-as-government-pumps-an-extra-15-billion-into-the-switch-to-electric) continues to take £1,500 or £3,750 off the price of eligible new models, and the list of qualifying cars keeps growing. Second, the Chancellor's announcement that EV owners will face a [3p-per-mile road charge from April 2028](https://theconversation.com/what-will-2026-look-like-for-the-uks-electric-vehicle-market-271441) has made running costs a live part of the conversation. In practice that is a small number per mile, but it marks the end of the idea that electric motoring is forever exempt from road funding. The Office for Budget Responsibility projects the charge will cost the market around 440,000 sales by 2031, partly offset by other measures in the budget.

Our take: none of this changes the maths for most households. A typical private driver covering 8,000 to 12,000 miles a year is looking at an extra £240 to £360 per year from 2028, set against fuel and maintenance savings that usually run into hundreds more than that. The bigger effect is psychological. Buyers are now comparing total cost of ownership properly, and the cars that survive that comparison tend to be the ones we would recommend anyway.

## Our recommendations by segment

Pricing below is current UK list pricing including the Electric Car Grant where it applies, cross-checked against [The Independent's best EVs list](https://the-independent.com/cars/electric-vehicles/best-electric-cars-uk-new-2026-b3024708.html) and [Nimblefins' 2026 buying guide](https://nimblefins.co.uk/best-electric-cars).

### City runabout: Renault 5 E-Tech

The best-selling new car in the UK right now, and it earns the position. From around £22,995 (roughly £21,495 after the grant on eligible trims), it offers up to 252 miles of WLTP range, charges at up to 100kW, and was named UK Car of the Year 2026. The honest caveat: rear seat space is tight for adults, so this is a car for couples and small families rather than regular four-adult journeys. If your budget does not stretch that far, the Leapmotor T03 from £14,495 and the Dacia Spring at around £12,240 after grant are credible budget alternatives for pure city use.

### Family hatchback: Kia EV3

The car to beat in 2026, with good reason. Priced from £33,055 up to £43,955 depending on battery and trim, the EV3 pairs an 81.4 kWh battery with a claimed range of up to 375 miles, charges at up to 128kW, and gives you a 460-litre boot that swallows a pram without ceremony. It won both the 2025 World Car of the Year and UK Car of the Year awards, which is rare enough that it deserves a mention. The tall, boxy shape divides opinion visually, but the space inside is exceptional for the class. If you want something more established, the Volkswagen ID.3 remains a strong runner-up at similar money.

### Family SUV: Skoda Elroq and Jaecoo E5

The Elroq is our first pick here. From around £30,210 after grant, it won Auto Express Car of the Year 2025 for exactly the reason most families want: it does not make you think about anything. A 470-litre boot, no-drama driving position, and practicality that carries over from Skoda's petrol range. The Jaecoo E5, third in the July registrations, is the value alternative at a lower price point with a bigger screen and more equipment per pound. It is a newer brand in this country, so plan to check the service network where you live before committing.

### Long range and larger families: BMW iX3 and Hyundai Ioniq 9

If range anxiety is your main objection to going electric, the BMW iX3 from £58,755 is the long-range benchmark in its class. For households that genuinely need seven seats, the Hyundai Ioniq 9 from £64,995 is the only large family EV we would seriously consider. Both sit well above the mainstream price bracket, and both should be test-driven against your actual driving pattern before purchase rather than bought on paper.

## The verdict

The electric car market in the UK has stopped being a question of whether and started being a question of which. The registrations say so: over a quarter of every new car sold this year is fully electric, and the used market is growing faster than any other segment in the country. Our advice for buyers is to start from how you drive rather than from the technology. A couple doing 10,000 miles a year in a city does not need a £65,000 Ioniq 9, and a family covering 25,000 with long commutes should look past the small cars entirely.

Match the segment to your life, check the running cost maths for your own mileage against the 2028 charge, and test drive at least two cars in the class you have settled on. That is how most of the top ten list got there anyway.
