The Home Office has revoked the type approval of the variable speed cameras used on England's smart motorways, a decision that takes all 154 of the affected HADECS3X units out of enforcement use and ends National Highways' contract with the US supplier Verra Mobility. Roads minister Justin Madders made the written statement to Parliament on [17 November](https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/national-highways-speed-enforcement-camera-issue), and the cameras stopped being usable as evidence from 18 November, as [Auto Express reported](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/news/370934/154-speed-cameras-deactivated-drivers-are-wrongly-fined).

The move follows a second fault found in the same camera system. The first, disclosed in December 2025, saw a programmed delay of 17.33 seconds between the variable speed limit signs and the cameras, producing at least 2,650 wrongful activations between 2021 and 2025 across 22 police force areas, as [the BBC detailed](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5y4j7z373kzo). The second fault, found during further testing this autumn, means the cameras could in specific rare circumstances activate against the wrong limit. National Highways' modelling puts the probability of that second error at no more than 0.1%, with no real-world evidence it has affected enforcement. The two faults sit in systems that date from a 2019 upgrade: Midas, which drives the variable speed limit signs, and HADECS3X, the camera system that enforces them, did not always communicate correctly when limits changed and changed back within a short window.

## What the review found

Alongside the statement, the Department for Transport published the independent Westall Review, led by Tracy Westall OBE, which examined how the original anomaly arose and how National Highways handled it, as [Fleet News summarised](https://www.fleetnews.co.uk/news/further-speed-camera-fault-found-as-national-highways-criticised). The review found ten missed opportunities to identify, investigate and escalate the issue after it was first flagged in January 2025, and concluded that poor governance, inadequate escalation procedures and delayed communication with the police turned a technical fault into a crisis. The original fault only came to light when a driver on the M5 challenged a Notice of Intended Prosecution with dashcam footage showing a 50mph limit displayed while the notice alleged a 40mph breach. The review makes recommendations across seven themes, from data and technology management through to procurement and contracting, and both the government and National Highways have accepted them.

## What changes for drivers

The 154 affected cameras cover all of England's smart motorways plus a section of the A14 between Huntingdon and Cambridge and the A1 approach to the A14, around 0.2% of England's roads. Their footage can no longer be used in prosecutions, so enforcement on those stretches now rests on the remaining 246 of the 400 adaptive cameras, the UK's several thousand other fixed and average speed cameras, police patrols, mobile cameras and ANPR. Variable speed signage continues to display as normal, and National Highways has added traffic officer patrols and CCTV monitoring at the affected sites. AA president Edmund King said that with the 154 cameras out of action around a million speeding events a year could go unpunished, and called for a new provider to be brought in quickly so a prolonged gap in enforcement does not become the accepted position. The Home Office concluded the issues could not be addressed to its satisfaction in any reasonable timeframe, which is what triggered the revocation; the camera system had been procured on the basis that it maintained that approval.

## Why it matters beyond the cameras

The case has become a reference point for how road enforcement technology is procured and governed, and it lands as cloned and ghost number plates are already at record levels. More than 11,400 drivers contacted the DVLA last year about fines for offences they did not commit, 9% more than in 2024 and almost double the 2018 figure, according to [RAC analysis of DVLA data](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/oct/16/cloned-number-plates-uk-roads-record-high) published in the Guardian on 16 October. The DVSA's own 2026/27 business plan, published on 16 November, promises a new fraud lab using drone technology and forensic analysis alongside the national ANPR service to tackle false number plates, which we covered in [our piece on the DVSA's plans for MOTs and EV testing](/dvsa-business-plan-2026-27-mot-adas-ev/). With cameras, plates and enforcement all under review at once, the smart motorway decision is as much about public confidence in the whole system as about one model of camera.

We have also covered the cost side of motoring this month in [the ONS's October inflation read on insurance and diesel](/ons-october-2026-insurance-8-percent-diesel-record/), where motor insurance rose 8% in the year to October.

For drivers, the practical points are simple. Variable limits on smart motorways remain legally binding, and enforcement continues through other means, but any prosecution resting solely on footage from an affected HADECS3X camera in the relevant period is open to challenge. Anyone wrongly fined under the first fault has already been reimbursed and had points removed. The redress process is still running for anyone identified later, and National Highways has said everyone it can contact has been offered redress. If you receive a notice for a smart motorway stretch and believe the camera was at fault, the dashcam route that exposed the original M5 case is the model to follow: keep the footage, challenge the notice in writing, and keep every reply.
