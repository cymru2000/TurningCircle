The Financial Conduct Authority has admitted to errors in the calculations behind its motor finance redress scheme, a day before two of the four legal challengers are due to serve expert evidence, and has asked Barclays to pause the early settlement offers it began making this month. The regulator says the mistakes change the average compensation by less than £10 per agreement, but acknowledges they affect the ongoing litigation. Lenders have set aside around £7.5bn for payouts under a scheme the FCA costed at £9.1bn including administration, covering an estimated 12.1m eligible agreements with an average redress of £829.

## What went wrong

While preparing its response to the legal challenges, the FCA [identified "some errors in how we categorised a small group of loan agreements"](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/statements/motor-finance-compensation-scheme-calculation) in the data behind the APR adjustment it uses to calculate compensation. That adjustment is set at 17% for agreements from 1 April 2014, and 21% for earlier ones. The regulator's initial work suggests any impact would be "less than 1 percentage point either side of 17%", which it puts at less than £10 per agreement on average against the £829 average redress it has estimated for the 12.1m eligible agreements.

The errors do not change who qualifies. The scheme still covers agreements taken out between 6 April 2007 and 1 November 2024 where commission was paid to the broker, with an estimated 37% of agreements eligible, according to the [FCA's consumer claims page](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/car-finance-complaints), updated on 26 November 2026. What the errors move is the arithmetic inside the calculation, not its scope. The 17% figure is not a penalty rate but an estimate of how much lower the APR on discretionary commission arrangement loans would have been under flat-fee terms, and it is the input the categorisation error fed into. The compensation itself is the average of that estimated loss and the commission paid, plus interest, subject to caps in around one in three cases.

## Why the timing matters

Two of the challengers, Volkswagen Financial Services and Mercedes-Benz Financial Services among the four named parties, are due to serve their expert evidence on Friday 27 November 2026. The FCA said it will discuss with the challengers and the court what the errors mean for that deadline and for the timetable of the litigation, which the Upper Tribunal is now listed to hear between 16 February and 3 March 2027, dates extended on 26 October from the original 16 to 26 February window. Our [explainer on the tribunal date](/fca-motor-finance-tribunal/) sets out what the hearing means for anyone with a PCP or HP agreement.

Until that process concludes, lenders do not have to calculate or pay compensation under the scheme. Parts of it have been suspended since 2 July 2026 on terms agreed with the challengers, which are Volkswagen Financial Services, Mercedes-Benz Financial Services, Credit Agricole Auto Finance and Consumer Voice, represented by Courmacs Legal.

## Barclays told to pause

Barclays Partner Finance, the only major lender to begin paying customers ahead of the scheme, has been asked by the FCA to pause its full and final settlement offers while the data issues are worked through. The bank began making offers on 3 November to some customers whose complaints were acknowledged on or before 1 October 2026, under conditions the FCA published the same morning, including that an offer must be at least what the scheme would pay and must give at least one month to respond. Our [report on those early settlements](/barclays-early-car-finance-settlements/) covers the terms customers were weighing up.

The pause does not unwind offers already accepted. An accepted settlement is full and final for commission complaints on that agreement, and a customer who takes the money keeps it whatever the tribunal later decides. What the pause stops is new offers going out on a calculation the regulator has just flagged as imperfect. Barclays had been making full and final offers using the FCA's own methodology, so the correction lands directly on the one payout route that was actually running.

## What it means for drivers

For anyone with a car finance agreement from the eligible window, the practical advice is unchanged and the FCA repeated it in the same statement: complain to your lender now, directly and for free, using the [template letter and lender contact list on the FCA's website](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/car-finance-complaints). A complaint lodged now sits behind the court case either way, but it is on record. The FCA said lenders are "substantially better prepared" than they were, having set aside around £7.5bn, and that motor finance lending rose 7% in the 12 months to September, so the market itself is not in question. Handling the same complaints one by one without a scheme was estimated to take three years and cost lenders around £6bn more than the scheme route. The open question is only how long the corrections add to a timetable that already runs into 2027.
