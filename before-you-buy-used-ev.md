The used EV market of 2026 is a different proposition from the one buyers got used to. For three years, prices slid while early adopters traded in and everyone else waited. That has now reversed. Used battery electric sales hit a record [110,761 transactions in the second quarter, up 67% on a year earlier](https://www.smmt.co.uk/used-car-market-returns-to-growth-as-ev-sales-accelerate/), and prices are climbing again: used EV values rose 3.3% year on year in July, the strongest annual growth on record, according to [Auto Trader data](https://cardealermagazine.co.uk/used-ev-prices-saw-record-annual-growth-in-july-as-autotrader-says-market-has-moved-decisively-beyond-correction/326289). A strengthening market is good news for sellers and a warning for buyers: the bargain-basement era is ending, so the checks you make before buying matter more than they did.

Here is what actually matters, in the order we would do them.

## Start with the battery, not the body

The battery is the expensive part of an electric car, so it is where diligence pays. The fear used to be that every used EV carried a tired pack one year from replacement. The data says otherwise. A UK study of more than 8,000 electric cars, [analysed by battery diagnostics firm Generational](https://evfleetworld.co.uk/average-used-ev-battery-health-stands-at-95-15-real-world-data-shows), found average state of health of 95.2%, with cars eight to twelve years old still holding a median of 85%. We went through that study and what it means in [our battery health guide](/battery-health-longevity/).

In practice: ask the seller for a battery health report before you view the car. Many specialists will run a diagnostic that reports state of health as a percentage. Anything above 90% on a car under six years old is unremarkable. If a seller cannot or will not produce a health report, treat that as information too.

Check the battery warranty as well. Most manufacturers cover the battery for eight years or 100,000 miles against excessive degradation, and that warranty attaches to the car, so a used example can still have years of cover left. Ask for the start date, which follows first registration, not your purchase.

## Run the history check, then read it

A full history check costs a few pounds and answers the questions that cost thousands if ignored: outstanding finance, write-off category, stolen-vehicle markers, and whether the mileage has been clocked. It is the same logic as any used car purchase, but electric cars add one wrinkle worth checking: make sure the vehicle identification matches the registration document and that the car has not been imported, because import history can complicate both warranty claims and resale.

## Confirm what comes in the box

Electric cars come with hardware that petrol cars do not, and replacing it out of your own pocket is expensive. Check the car has its charging cables, both the slow granny cable for a domestic socket and the Type 2 cable for public and wallbox charging, and that they are the correct ones for that model. Ask whether the car still has access to any bundled public-charging account or tariff, because some manufacturers include charging credits that do not always transfer but are worth asking about.

## Match the range to your actual driving

Ignore the dashboard's maximum range figure and work backwards from your life instead. The average British driver covers [around 6,100 miles a year, and 70% of trips are under five miles](/how-far-do-uk-drivers-drive/), which nearly every used EV on sale handles comfortably. The question is your longest regular day, not your average one. A car with 150 real-world miles of range suits a household that does 40 miles a day with weekend trips, and suits nobody who regularly drives 200 miles without stopping. Work out your weekly mileage before viewing anything, then buy the range you need rather than the range you fear.

## Test drive it like you own it

Take any candidate on the fastest road you regularly use. Electric cars are silent at 30 mph, which hides tyre wear, bearing noise and wind noise that show up at 70. Feel for whether the regenerative braking behaves consistently, check that the touchscreen and its critical controls respond without lag, and if the car can precondition and route-plan, test that those functions work, because on older software they are the difference between an easy long trip and a stressful one.

## Price it against a moving market

Prices are no longer falling, so the old advice to wait another six months no longer guarantees a better deal. Values for three-to-five-year-old EVs [rose 8.9% in the latest Auto Trader data](https://www.am-online.com/news/used-ev-prices-return-to-growth-after-three-year-slide), and stock is turning faster, which means the car you defer on may be gone. Compare against [what similar cars are actually selling for](/ev-depreciation-residual-values/), not against the price you remember from 2024, and remember that record used sales mean you are buying into a market with a growing buyer base behind you, which protects the value of whatever you buy.

## The short version

The used EV market has stopped being a clearance sale and started being a normal market, which changes the buying discipline: verify the battery rather than assuming the worst, run the history check without exception, confirm the hardware, and buy the range your week actually needs. Do those four things and the strongest argument for a used electric car is now the same as it always should have been: the numbers, not the discount.
