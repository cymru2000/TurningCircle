More than one car in four turns up to its MOT carrying a fault serious enough to fail. That is not a slogan from a garage chain; it is the measured result across every one of the [33,310,534 class 3 and 4 tests recorded in Great Britain between April 2025 and March 2026](https://www.gov.uk/csv-preview/66c867661f215d9f0a91353f/dvsa-mot-01-mot-test-results-by-class-of-vehicle.csv), where the initial failure rate came in at 27.51%. After faults fixed at the test station during the same visit, the final failure rate settled at 22.30%. Both figures come from the DVSA's own statistical data set, last updated on 10 September 2026, and both are worth knowing because they answer different questions: the first is what the car arrived with, the second is what it left as.

The same data answers the question drivers actually ask, which is what they are most likely to be pulled up on. The answer splits into two lists that do not line up, and the gap between them is the useful part.

## The most common failures

By share of all tests, the defect categories that fail cars most often in 2025-26 were [lamps, reflectors and electrical equipment on 10.44% of tests, suspension on 8.90%, tyres on 6.23%, brakes on 6.10% and visibility on 4.28%](https://www.gov.uk/csv-preview/649ae34db4d6ef000c038f86/dvsa-mot-03-mot-class-3-and-4-vehicles-initial-failures-by-defect-category.csv). Measured as a share of failure defects rather than tests, the order is the same: lamps at 24.89%, suspension at 20.49%, brakes at 15.18%, tyres at 12.94%, visibility at 7.72%. One car can fail in several categories at once, which is why the percentages add up to more than the 27.51% headline; the average initial failure carried 2.39 defects.

The lighting figure is the one that surprises people. A blown bulb or a failed number-plate lamp is the single biggest reason a car fails, which is also why it is the cheapest to prevent. Our [guide to the LED bulb rules that took effect in October](/mot-led-bulb-rules/) explains what testers must now accept in halogen headlamps, and what still fails regardless.

## The most dangerous failures

Frequency and danger are different rankings. When testers recorded a dangerous defect, meaning the car should not be driven away as it was, [tyres accounted for 60.60% of those dangerous defects and brakes for a further 28.80%](https://www.gov.uk/csv-preview/649ae34db4d6ef000c038f86/dvsa-mot-03-mot-class-3-and-4-vehicles-initial-failures-by-defect-category.csv), between them nearly nine in ten. Overall, 7.22% of all class 3 and 4 tests found at least one dangerous item. Lamps, the most common failure, made up just 1.27% of dangerous defects, which is the opposite story: frequent, but rarely serious.

Tyres overtook brakes in the failure table this year, but read the trend before the ranking. Both improved: tyre faults fell from 6.39% of tests a year earlier to 6.23%, and brake faults from 6.63% to 6.10%. Brakes simply improved faster. The dangerous-defect share is the number to watch: a dangerous tyre defect was recorded on 4.47% of all tests, roughly one in 22 cars arriving with a tyre it should not have been driving on.

The legal minimum tread depth is 1.6mm across the central three-quarters of the tread and around the whole circumference. The 20p test is a quick screen: push a 20p coin into the main grooves and if the outer band stays visible, the tread may be below the limit.

## Passing with a warning is still passing

A valid MOT does not mean a sound car, and the gap is measurable. TyreSafe's analysis of DVSA test records, published on 12 November 2026, found that [almost 7 million cars, 6,993,077 to be exact, passed their MOT in 2025 carrying at least one tyre advisory](https://www.tyresafe.org/about-tyresafe/latest-news/7-million-cars-passed-their-mot-last-year-with-a-tyre-warning-a-valid-mot-is-not-enough-for-grey-fleet-says-tyresafe/). Those cars were almost twice as likely to present a dangerous tyre defect at their next test, 7.4% against 3.8%, and more than a third, 36.8%, of car tyre failures had been flagged with an advisory at the previous MOT. Cars covering more than 15,000 miles a year were 2.6 times as likely to have a dangerous tyre at the next test as cars covering under 7,000, even with no prior advisory.

That matters beyond the annual test. Our [guide to how far UK drivers actually drive](/how-far-do-uk-drivers-drive/) puts the average at just over 6,000 miles a year, so a car doing double that is in the wear band where advisories turn into dangerous defects inside twelve months.

## What an MOT costs, and what it does not cover

The test fee itself is capped. The [maximum a test station may charge for a class 4 car is £54.85](https://www.gov.uk/getting-an-mot/mot-test-fees), a figure unchanged since April 2010, and £29.65 for a standard motorcycle. Across standalone MOTs booked through BookMyGarage between October 2025 and September 2026, the average price actually paid was £47.61, with half of drivers paying £49 or less, so most garages sit below the cap. The fee buys the inspection only; it does not include any repair needed to pass, and a car that fails for a dangerous defect can be fined up to £2,500, banned from driving and given three penalty points if driven.

The fail rate has been drifting down for years, from 29.21% in 2021-22 to 27.51% in 2025-26, on rising test volumes. The latest quarter, April to June 2026, recorded a 26.75% initial fail rate on 7,808,594 class 3 and 4 tests. Whatever the year, the practical takeaway is the same: check the lights, wipers and tyre tread before the test, and read the advisories on a pass as carefully as a failure, because that is where the next dangerous defect is already written.
