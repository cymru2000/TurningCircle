Agratas has lost the second lead construction contractor on its £5.2bn battery gigafactory at Bridgwater, Somerset, in five months, after TSL, formally Tonroe Group, failed to agree terms with the Tata-owned battery business over the project's timeline and cost. The [Guardian reported on 23 November](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/nov/23/flagship-uk-gigafactory-turmoil-loses-second-builder) that TSL is expected to leave the site within two or three months, with no other main build contractor lined up, although NG Bailey, the project's mechanical and electrical contractor, is being considered to take over the whole build.

The plant is being built to supply batteries to Agratas's sister company Jaguar Land Rover, Britain's largest automotive employer, and both firms sit within India's Tata Group. TSL replaced Sir Robert McAlpine, which [Agratas removed at short notice in June](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/jun/20/jaguar-land-rover-jlr-battery-supply-delay-somerset-agratas) after three weeks' notice, and the Guardian reported that discussions over the terms of that departure are still ongoing. Construction trade coverage of the latest exit, from [Construction Enquirer on 23 November](https://constructionenquirer.com/2026/11/23/tsl-to-exit-somerset-battery-plant-job/), says NG Bailey has already absorbed earlier packages on the scheme and is now being lined up for the final phase of Building One. The Bridgwater site is part of Tata's wider Gravity Smart Campus, and the first of three planned production buildings is the one now awaiting a finish contractor.

## The January 2028 date is still the official target

When Tata announced the gigafactory in 2023 it aimed for a 2026 start of production, with as many as 4,200 employees, before pushing the date to 2027 and then to January 2028. Agratas has not moved that latest date. An Agratas spokesperson told the Guardian: "We are on track to meet our customer requirements. The Somerset site is a large and complex industrialisation project, and all of our supplier partners are supporting us well as we prepare for the facility's launch phase." The 40GWh plant was billed in 2024 as Britain's biggest battery factory.

The UK government has [pledged £380m in financial support](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/apr/09/somerset-battery-factory-jaguar-land-rover-government-funding) for the factory because it is seen as crucial to the long-term survival of the British car industry, and the Guardian reported that not all of it has been paid out. The shell of Building One is largely complete and National Grid has begun work on the substation that will supply cell production, but the loss of a second lead builder in five months adds to doubts over whether the January 2028 target can hold without a replacement contractor in place. Agratas's own statement, carried by [Building magazine on 23 November](https://www.building.co.uk/news/second-contractor-set-to-leave-giant-car-battery-site-in-somerset/5145312.article), describes the plant as due to be operational from next year and keeps the official line that the company is on track.

## What it means for JLR and British-made batteries

JLR has secured batteries from other suppliers, so its plans to increase sales of electric Land Rovers and Jaguars are not expected to be affected, the Guardian reported. The first of those cars will therefore not carry British-made batteries. Using batteries made in Europe could still let JLR avoid EU tariffs under rules of origin, which matters as the EU-UK summit on 22 November left the January 2027 EV tariff start unchanged, as we covered in [/eu-uk-summit-no-ev-tariff-delay-january-2027/](/eu-uk-summit-no-ev-tariff-delay-january-2027/).

The Bridgwater setback compounds a wider squeeze on UK battery capacity. The country's only operating gigafactory, AESC's plant in Sunderland next to Nissan's factory, [shelved its expansion plans in August](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/15/uk-ev-battery-gigafactory-aesc-jaguar-land-rover-nissan) after failing to agree a supply deal with JLR, and is now aiming for annual output of 15.8GWh, enough for about 300,000 electric cars. That leaves British car makers dependent on imported cells for longer than planned, at a moment when JLR is cutting 4,000 jobs and reporting a £451m quarterly loss, as we reported in [/jlr-q2-fy27-451m-loss-operating-profit/](/jlr-q2-fy27-451m-loss-operating-profit/).

## Why the contractor churn keeps happening

Construction Enquirer's earlier reporting on the June changeover shows the programme had already slipped: Agratas redesigned and scaled back the first of three planned production buildings after the piling phase, pushing first-phase completion from 2026 into 2027, and M&E contractor TClarke walked away in March over relationship problems with the client. The pattern of exits, first McAlpine, then TClarke, now TSL, points to a project whose cost and schedule assumptions keep shifting under the client. Internal strain has also been reported, with some Agratas staff asked to relocate from Coventry to Somerset and expected to take redundancy instead. Each change of lead builder also resets the learning curve on a site where the substation, clean rooms and cell production lines still have to be commissioned before the first batteries can roll out.

For the UK car industry the practical question is narrower: how many electric cars built in Britain over the next two years will carry cells from Bridgwater rather than from European or Asian suppliers. With the January 2028 date unmoved but no lead builder in place, the honest answer is that the plant's first batteries are more likely to arrive late than on schedule.
