Autumn is usually the quiet season for new car shows, but the affordable end of the electric market has spent the past month getting cheaper. The Electric Car Grant now covers more than 35 models, its funding is guaranteed to 2030, and two of the most interesting cars on the shelf, the updated Renault 5 and the Nissan Pixo, made their moves in the past fortnight. If you are shopping this autumn, here is how the scheme stands and what the money actually buys.

We keep a fuller walkthrough of how the discount works in [our guide to the grant list](/grant-eligible-cars-list/) and whether it is changing behaviour in [our look at the grant's track record](/does-the-grant-actually-work/). This is the dated snapshot: what the shelf looks like on 1 October 2026.

## How the bands work now

The grant is a discount applied at the point of sale on new eligible electric cars, and it comes in two bands. Band one pays £3,750, band two pays £1,500, and eligibility runs to cars priced at or below £37,000 recommended retail price, per [the government's eligibility guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/how-to-apply-for-vehicle-eligibility-for-the-electric-car-grant/how-to-apply-for-vehicle-eligibility-for-the-electric-car-grant), which was last updated on 1 April 2026. Which band a car lands in depends on the carbon footprint of its production, from battery manufacture through assembly to the carbon intensity of the grid where it is built. The cleaner the production, the higher the band.

The scheme's finances were settled at last November's Budget, which added £1.3bn to take the total to £2bn and extended it to 2030, as [edie reported at the time](https://edie.net/budget-chancellor-confirms-pay-per-mile-ev-tax-and-funding-boost-for-chargers). By December 2025 the grant had supported more than 40,000 drivers, per [the government's own announcement](https://automotiveworld.com/news/uk-expands-electric-car-grant-to-four-new-models) of the December expansion, and no more recent total has been published since. The list has kept growing: it passed 35 models this autumn and, at the time of writing, [Autocar's running tally](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-news/electric-cars/all-cars-eligible-uks-electric-car-grant) lists more than 60 models across the two bands.

## What £3,750 buys this autumn

The top band is where the value sits, and it is crowded. Autocar's list currently has the Kia EV2 Long Range at £27,995, or £24,245 after grant, the Fiat 500e from £20,995, or £17,245, the Ford Puma Gen-E at £28,999, or £25,249, and the Mini Countryman Electric at £32,805, or £29,255. The Renault 5's 52kWh version qualifies too, and Renault has gone a step further by folding the grant into its headline prices outright: the updated R5 opened for UK orders on 24 September with the entry 40kWh car unchanged at £21,495 on the road and the 52kWh Comfort Range at £23,945, both inclusive of the £3,750, as [electrive reported](https://electrive.com/2026/09/24/uk-orders-open-for-updated-renault-5-e-tech/). Range on those two is now 196 and 267 miles respectively.

Two entries deserve a special mention because of what they say about manufacturing. The Nissan Leaf, built in Sunderland, takes the full £3,750, bringing it to £32,249 in the trim Autocar lists. Meanwhile the investment context around UK plants keeps improving, with Nissan's £170m Sunderland commitment and McLaren's £500m South Yorkshire plan in September, which we covered in [our piece on the two announcements](/mclaren-nissan-investment/). The grant's production-sustainability rules do not explicitly favour British-built cars, but the UK is doing disproportionately well out of them so far.

## What £1,500 buys, and where the gap bites

Band two is wider and, on the numbers, weaker. The Citroën ë-C3 sits at £22,095, or £20,595 after grant, the Vauxhall Corsa Electric, the Kia EV3 and the Volkswagen ID 3 all appear here, and several models within £1,000 of the price cap missed the top band entirely on production carbon. That £2,250 difference between bands is now doing real work in the market: two nearly identical cars at nearly identical list prices can end up £2,250 apart at the till, and buyers are learning to check the band before the brochure.

The pattern worth noticing is who clusters where. The Renault group's small cars, built in France on the shared AmpR Small platform, dominate the top band's affordable end. Stellantis models split between bands. And the cheapest new electric car on the horizon is not on the list at all yet.

## The December question: the Pixo

The Nissan Pixo was revealed on 23 September as the brand's return to the city-car segment: a 27.5kWh LFP battery, up to 161 miles of WLTP range, 50kW DC charging, and prices and pre-orders to be announced "from December onwards", per [electrive's reveal coverage](https://www.electrive.com/2026/09/23/nissan-pixo-an-electric-return-to-the-a-segment/). Its French sister car, the Twingo, starts under £20,000 in equivalent terms, and the expectation is that the Pixo lands close to that, but Nissan has confirmed nothing. If it qualifies for band one, a sub-£20,000 electric car becomes £16,250 after grant, which would be the lowest effective new EV price the UK market has seen. That is a possibility, not a promise, and it depends on two things nobody has confirmed: the price and the band.

We set out what the Pixo is and is not in [our piece on its reveal](/nissan-pixo-revealed/), and we will treat every price attached to it as unconfirmed until Nissan publishes one.

## The practical briefing

For an autumn buyer, the sequence is simple. Check the current list, because models are added every few weeks and a car rejected in July can be approved in October. Check the band, because the £2,250 gap decides most value comparisons at the affordable end. Check whether the advertised price already includes the grant, as Renault's now does, or sits above it. And if your budget is under £20,000, it may be worth waiting until December to see what the Pixo lands at, with the Fiat 500e at £17,245 after grant as the current benchmark to beat.

The grant is no longer a pilot or a promise. It is £2bn of committed money running to 2030, and the shelf it has created is the most affordable the new electric market has ever been.
