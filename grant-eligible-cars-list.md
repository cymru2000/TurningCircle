The Electric Car Grant has quietly become one of the most generous purchase incentives on the British market. Since it launched in July 2025 it has taken thousands of pounds off new electric cars, and the list of qualifying models keeps growing. The catch is that not every EV qualifies, and the difference between the two discount levels is large: £3,750 in the top band, £1,500 in the second. If you are shopping for a new electric car right now, knowing which models carry which discount is worth more than any negotiation you will have at the dealership.

Here is the list as it stands, how the scheme works, and the details that catch buyers out.

## How the grant works

The mechanics are simple. The grant is applied automatically by the dealer at the point of sale, so there is no form to fill in and no claim to make. The discount comes straight off the purchase price. To qualify at all, a car must be a brand new passenger vehicle with zero tailpipe emissions, a minimum range of 100 miles, a 3-year or 60,000 mile vehicle warranty, an 8-year or 100,000 mile battery warranty, and it must [meet minimum sustainability criteria](https://www.gov.uk/zero-emission-vehicle-grants/cars) covering how it and its battery are made.

The price rules matter more than most buyers realise. A model qualifies if its [starting price is £37,000 or less](https://www.which.co.uk/reviews/new-and-used-cars/article/electric-car-grant-all-eligible-models-and-other-brand-discounts-a42Mi7y8tDek), and higher-spec versions of the same car can still qualify up to £42,000 if they share the same battery and motor combination as an eligible cheaper version. That is why some trims of a model carry the grant and others do not, a trap we come back to below.

The discount itself is banded by how sustainably the car is built, judged largely on manufacturing emissions and whether the manufacturer holds a verified science-based emissions target. The cleanest-built cars land in Band 1 with £3,750 off; the rest of the eligible field sits in Band 2 with £1,500 off.

## The details that catch buyers out

Three things are worth checking before you sign anything. The first is the variant. Several models only qualify in specific battery or trim versions: the Renault 5 needs the 52kWh battery for the full grant, the Nissan Micra needs the 52kWh pack likewise, and the Citroën ë-C5 Aircross needs the Long Range version. The cheaper variants of those cars sit in Band 2 instead. Ask the dealer to confirm the grant on the exact car, in writing, as part of the quote.

The second is the price ceiling. Options can push a car over the line, since the £42,000 extension only applies to versions sharing the same battery and motor as a sub-£37,000 model. A winter pack or a panoramic roof will not break eligibility on its own, but a different motor will.

The third is timing. The government has said the scheme runs to 31 March 2030, supported by [an extra £1.3 billion announced at the Autumn Budget, taking total funding to around £2 billion](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/four-more-ev-models-qualify-for-3750-discount-under-electric-car-grant-as-government-pumps-an-extra-15-billion-into-the-switch-to-electric), and it can be amended without notice. Over 40,000 drivers had used the grant by December 2025, and uptake has accelerated since. The list also moves in both directions: models join as manufacturers complete the sustainability assessments, and eligibility is reassessed when pricing changes.

## Is the grant actually worth it?

On the numbers, yes, especially at the top band. £3,750 is a bigger discount than most manufacturers offer on an equivalent petrol model, and it stacks on top of any dealer finance contributions. Combined with the lower running costs of electric motoring, which we have covered in [why EVs are cheaper to service](/why-evs-are-cheaper-to-service/) and [fuel duty versus the electric mile](/fuel-duty-vs-electric-mile/), a grant-eligible EV is one of the better value propositions in the new car market right now.

The strategic question, whether the grant is genuinely changing what people buy or just subsidising sales that would have happened anyway, is a live one, and we will return to it. For the individual buyer the calculation is simpler. If the car you want is on the Band 1 list, the grant is a substantial saving you do not have to work for. If it is in Band 2, the £1,500 is still worth having, but it should not be the deciding factor between two close rivals. Choose the car first, then let the grant follow.
