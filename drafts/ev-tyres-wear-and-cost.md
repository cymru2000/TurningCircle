The servicing bill on an electric car is genuinely smaller than on a petrol one: no oil, no filters, no spark plugs, no cambelt. Tyres are the line item that claws part of that saving back. An electric car is heavier, its torque arrives instantly, and its regenerative braking puts load through the tyres in a different way. The result is that many owners replace tyres sooner than they expected, and at prices that surprise them.

The good news is that the wear is predictable and partly controllable, and that the tyre market has caught up with the cars. The bad news is that "EV tyre" is now a marketing term as much as a technical one, and not every tyre carrying the label is worth the premium.

## Why an electric car eats rubber

Three forces do the damage.

The first is mass. A battery pack adds several hundred kilograms compared with the combustion drivetrain it replaces, and every extra kilogram rests on four contact patches roughly the size of a hand. Heavier cars wear tyres faster, brake later and generate more heat in the sidewall, and the effect compounds on a large electric SUV.

The second is torque delivery. Maximum pulling force is available the instant the motor turns, which is what makes an electric car feel quick and what makes it hard on the driven tyres. The same journey driven gently and driven briskly can differ by tens of thousands of miles over the life of a set, and the difference is almost entirely in how the accelerator is used from a standstill.

The third is regeneration. Lifting off transfers braking force to the driven wheels rather than spreading it across all four, and it does so constantly in town driving. That is good for brake discs and less good for tyre shoulders, which is why front tyres on a front-driven electric car often wear on the outer edge before the centre.

The pattern shows up in the data. Our [analysis of used EV reliability](/used-ev-reliability-study/) found electric cars recording roughly 1.8 times as many tyre defects as petrol cars in MOT results, with the problem most pronounced on earlier models adapted from combustion platforms. That is a defect rate rather than a wear rate, but the two are related: tyres that reach their limit sooner are tyres that get flagged sooner.

## What "EV-specific" actually means

An EV tyre is not a different category in law. It is a set of design choices, and they trade against each other.

The defining feature is low rolling resistance, because rolling resistance is one of the largest remaining losses in an electric car and every watt saved extends the range. The compound is usually stiffer and the tread pattern shallower than a performance tyre's, which helps efficiency and can cost a little grip. Better EV tyres compensate with a higher wet-grip rating rather than accepting the trade, which is why the EU tyre label is worth reading: an A wet-grip rating on a low-rolling-resistance tyre is the combination to look for.

Beyond that, EV tyres commonly carry a higher load index to cope with the battery's weight, reinforced sidewalls, and acoustic foam inside the carcass to reduce the road noise that becomes obvious once there is no engine to mask it. Some are self-sealing. None of that is exclusive to electric cars, and a conventional tyre with the right load rating will often do the job, particularly on a smaller, lighter EV.

The real question is not whether a tyre says EV on the sidewall. It is whether the load index meets the car's requirement, whether the wet grip is good, and whether the price premium buys anything you will notice.

## The numbers: tread, cost and life

The legal minimum in the UK is 1.6mm of tread across the central three-quarters of the tyre around its whole circumference. That is the threshold at which an MOT fails, not the threshold at which a tyre stops working. Wet braking performance degrades well before the legal limit, which is why TyreSafe and most manufacturers recommend replacing at 3mm. The difference between 1.6mm and 3mm in standing water is the difference between stopping and arriving, and it is not a close call.

Cost depends mostly on wheel size, and electric cars tend towards larger wheels for range and aesthetics. Budget around £90 to £140 per tyre fitted for a 17- or 18-inch EV, £130 to £200 for 19- and 20-inch sizes, and more for the low-profile fitments on larger SUVs. Four tyres on a family electric SUV can comfortably exceed £700 fitted, which is why the wear rate matters more than the unit price.

Realistic life is harder to pin down than the internet suggests. A gently driven small EV on 17-inch tyres may see 30,000 to 40,000 miles from a set. A heavier SUV driven briskly, with a lot of town regeneration and a lot of torque, may need fronts at 18,000 to 25,000 miles. The variable is the driver far more than the tyre.

## Rotation, alignment and pressures

Three cheap habits extend tyre life more than any purchase decision.

Rotate the tyres. On a car with staggered sizes rotation is not possible, but on a square setup swapping front to rear at 6,000 to 8,000 mile intervals evens out the wear, and on a front-driven EV the fronts do most of the work. Many owners never do this because the service schedule no longer includes an oil change to prompt them.

Check the pressures monthly, cold, against the plate in the door jamb rather than the number on the sidewall. Electric cars often run higher pressures than drivers expect, and underinflation does double damage: it increases rolling resistance, costing range, and it wears the shoulders. It also generates heat, which is the mechanism behind most high-speed tyre failures.

Get the alignment checked after any significant pothole strike, and annually in any case. Electric cars are heavy enough that a knock which would have been harmless on a hatchback can put the geometry out, and misalignment shows up as uneven inner-edge wear that is invisible until the tyre is scrap. It is also the single most common reason a tyre fails early with tread left in the middle.

## Punctures, and the missing spare

Most electric cars are sold without a spare wheel, for weight and packaging reasons. What they carry instead varies: a sealant kit, a compressor, run-flat tyres, or in some cases nothing at all beyond a recovery number.

A sealant kit handles a simple tread puncture and nothing else. It cannot repair a sidewall, a shoulder or a tear, and a tyre that has been run flat on sealant is often unrepairable afterwards because the sidewall has been damaged by the deflation. A proper internal patch and plug, fitted to BS AU 159, is a permanent repair and costs far less than a new tyre, but it requires the tyre to be removed and inspected.

The practical advice for an EV owner is to know which system the car has before the first puncture, and to treat a slow loss of pressure as something to investigate the same day. A heavy car on a soft tyre overheats quickly, and the difference between a £35 repair and a £200 replacement is often a matter of hours.

## Buying used: what to check

Tyres are the cheapest thing to inspect and the most expensive thing to overlook. On any used electric car, check all four for matching brand and specification, since a mismatch suggests a budget replacement after damage. Check tread across the full width, not just the centre, because inner-edge wear indicates alignment problems that will destroy the new tyres too. Look for cracking in the sidewall on low-mileage older cars, where the rubber has aged rather than worn out.

Then check the pressures and the spare or kit, and look at the wheels for kerb damage that might indicate a harder life than the mileage suggests. A set of four tyres at 3mm is an immediate £500 bill on a larger car, and that is a negotiating point rather than a reason to walk away.

## The honest maths

Tyres cost an electric car owner more than the servicing saving recovers, but the gap is smaller than the internet's version of events. A set every 30,000 miles at £500 fitted is about 1.7p a mile, against perhaps 3p a mile for the fuel saved on the same journey. The saving still wins; the tyres simply take a bite out of it.

What tilts the maths is driver behaviour and maintenance. Rotating, checking pressures, fixing alignment and buying on wet grip rather than on the EV label will between them turn a 20,000-mile set into a 30,000-mile set. That is worth more than any tyre marketing claim, and it costs nothing but attention.
