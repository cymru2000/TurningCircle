Electric cars are leased in larger numbers than petrol cars, and the reasons are structural rather than fashionable: the technology moves quickly, residual values have been volatile, and a monthly payment insulates the driver from both. The consequence is that a growing number of people reach the end of a contract having never owned the car, and discover in the final three months that the end of a lease is a set of decisions with money attached.

The decisions themselves are simple. What makes them awkward is that two of the three were made at the start, in the small print of the mileage allowance and the condition standard, and one of them depends on a market that may have moved since.

## First, work out which contract you have

Personal contract hire and personal contract purchase look similar on a quote and behave completely differently at the end.

With contract hire, you are renting the car. There is no option to buy it, no final payment, and no equity. At the end of the term you hand it back, pay any excess mileage and any chargeable damage, and walk away. The monthly payment is usually lower precisely because the risk of the car's future value sits with the funder.

With a PCP, you are buying the car on credit with a large final payment deferred. That deferred amount, the guaranteed minimum future value, was set at the start. At the end you have three choices: pay the balloon and keep the car, part-exchange it against another car and use any equity as a deposit, or hand it back and owe nothing provided the mileage and condition terms are met.

The distinction matters because the hand-back rules that people complain about apply to both, while the equity question applies only to the PCP. On a car whose value has fallen further than the guaranteed figure, there is no equity to use and handing back is often the rational move. On a car worth more, the difference is yours to spend. Electric residuals have been unpredictable enough that this is genuinely a live question rather than a formality, which is why our [guide to EV depreciation](/ev-depreciation-residual-values/) reads the data rather than the rumour.

## The optional final payment

If you have a PCP, the balloon is not a bill that arrives by surprise, though it often feels like one. It is a fixed number in the agreement, and the decision is arithmetic.

Compare the balloon with what the car is actually worth at that mileage and age. If the balloon is £14,000 and the car is worth £16,000, paying it and selling privately or part-exchanging captures roughly £2,000 of equity. If the balloon is £16,000 and the car is worth £14,000, paying it means writing a cheque for more than the asset is worth, and handing back is the cleaner exit. A third route exists in the middle: refinance the balloon with a loan, which converts the decision into a question about interest rates rather than car values.

The mistake to avoid is signing the balloon payment automatically because the car is familiar. A used electric car at three years old may be worth considerably less than its guaranteed value, and loyalty to a depreciating asset is expensive.

## Fair wear and tear, and what actually gets charged

Every lease sets a condition standard, and in the UK the reference point for most funders is the BVRLA's Fair Wear and Tear Standard. It distinguishes between damage consistent with normal use, which is not chargeable, and damage beyond it, which is.

The categories are predictable. Stone chips and light scratching on the front bumper are normal. Kerbed alloy wheels beyond a defined size, dents, torn trim, cracked lights, mismatched or excessively worn tyres, and damage from unrepaired collisions are not. The thresholds matter and they are specific, which is why the first practical step is to ask the funder for its condition guide and read the examples before the inspection rather than after the invoice.

Two details catch electric car drivers in particular. The first is tyres: a lease requires them to be road legal and matched, and the instant torque of an electric car makes front tyre wear quicker than drivers expect. Returning a car on 2mm of tread is a chargeable defect, and replacing tyres yourself before the inspection is almost always cheaper than the funder's replacement rate.

The second is equipment. Charging cables, the granny cable if one was supplied, the keys and any service records are part of the car. Missing cables are charged at list price, and a replacement key can run to several hundred pounds once coding is included. Put all of it in the boot on the day.

Wear items you are responsible for during the contract, servicing and tyres among them, do not become the funder's problem at the end. A missed service can be chargeable even when the car is otherwise immaculate, because it affects the resale value.

## Excess mileage

The mileage allowance is a price, not a suggestion. Contracts typically set a per-mile charge in the region of 6p to 15p, applied to every mile over the total allowance rather than to the monthly average.

The arithmetic is unforgiving at the margin. A 10,000-mile-a-year contract over three years allows 30,000 miles. Returning it at 36,000 miles with a 10p rate produces a £600 bill, which can exceed the entire deposit. Drivers who underestimated their mileage, which is common for anyone who switched to an electric car and started volunteering for the longer journeys because the running costs were so low, should check their position six months out rather than at the end. Some funders will adjust the allowance mid-contract for a fee; the same adjustment at hand-back is not available.

There is one legitimate mitigation: the charge applies to the total, so a driver who is over at month 30 but stops using the car may still land under. Check the projected total, not the current one.

## Extension, early termination, and the escape routes

If the next car has not arrived, or the market is unappealing, most funders will extend. Ask three months before the end, in writing. Extensions are commonly offered for one to six months at the existing monthly payment, sometimes with a small administration fee, and the mileage allowance is usually pro-rated. Extending is almost always cheaper than an informal holdover, which some contracts price punitively.

Ending early is harder. On a PCP, the Consumer Credit Act gives a voluntary termination right once half of the total amount payable has been paid, which returns the car and ends the agreement without further payments, subject to condition and mileage. That right does not exist on a pure hire contract, where early termination means a settlement figure calculated from the remaining rentals, often with a substantial early termination charge.

If a car is written off, the finance does not disappear. The insurer pays market value, and any shortfall between that and the settlement figure remains the driver's debt unless gap insurance was taken. This is the scenario in which a lease goes badly wrong, and it is worth knowing before rather than after.

## If you disagree with the inspection

End-of-contract inspections are carried out by contractors, and disputes are common enough to have a procedure.

Ask for the report in writing, with photographs, at the time of inspection. Challenge specific items in writing to the funder, referencing the BVRLA standard and the condition guide. If the funder does not resolve it, the complaint escalates: to the Financial Ombudsman Service for the credit agreement, and, where the dispute is about the vehicle or a dealer's conduct rather than the finance, to the Motor Ombudsman. We covered the shape of that landscape, and its limits, in [our piece on the Motor Ombudsman at ten](/motor-ombudsman-decade-adr-gap/).

The complaints route is free and slow, which means the practical advice is to photograph everything yourself before the car is collected, including the odometer, the tyres and every panel. Evidence taken on the day is worth more than an argument three weeks later.

## The last three months, in order

Sixty days out, request the settlement or balloon figure and the condition guide. Check the projected mileage against the allowance. Get two valuations, one from the funder's own route and one from a dealer or an online buyer, so the equity question is answered with numbers rather than hope. Decide between keeping, part-exchanging and handing back, and get any extension agreed in writing. Replace worn tyres and gather the cables, keys and service records. Photograph the car.

Then, on the day, treat the inspection as an evidence exercise rather than a formality. The lease was always going to end this way; the only variable is how much the final invoice costs, and most of that is decided by what you did in the preceding three months.
