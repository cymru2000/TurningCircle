Electric cars tow well. That sentence surprises people who assume the battery is a liability, but the mechanical case is straightforward: instant torque at zero rpm, a low centre of gravity, and a heavy kerbweight that stabilises a trailer. What makes towing with an EV different is not the pulling. It is the paperwork, the weight plate, and the fact that a battery asked to move two tonnes of car plus a caravan empties at roughly half its usual rate.

## The licence question, settled

The rules changed at the end of 2021 and most drivers still describe the old ones. Since 16 December 2021, a standard category B licence allows you to drive a car and tow a trailer with a maximum authorised mass of up to 3,500kg. The previous combined vehicle-and-trailer limit of 3,500kg was removed at the same time.

In practice that covers almost every caravan and car trailer a private buyer will consider, and it removed the need for most people to take a B+E test. What it did not do is lift the car's own limits: a category B licence permits up to 3,500kg of trailer, but the car's towing capacity may be far lower, and the lower of the two governs. Anything above 3,500kg moves into the goods vehicle categories, which is a different conversation entirely.

The other figure people quote is the 85% guidance, which suggests a trailer's maximum laden mass should not exceed 85% of the car's kerbweight. It is advice from the caravan organisations, not law, and it is aimed at stability rather than legality. It remains a sensible starting point for a first caravan, particularly with a heavy electric car whose kerbweight flatters the ratio.

## The weights that matter more than the motor

Three numbers live on the car's weight plate and in the handbook, and all three need checking before you buy a towbar, let alone a trailer.

The first is the braked towing capacity, which for electric cars ranges from around 750kg on smaller models to 2,500kg on large electric SUVs. The second is the unbraked limit, usually 750kg, which is the practical ceiling for a small box trailer or a dinghy. The third is the noseweight, typically between 50kg and 100kg, which is how much downward load the towbar can take. Exceeding noseweight is one of the most common causes of poor towing behaviour, and it is invisible until the trailer starts to sway.

Trailers over 750kg must have their own brakes, and in the UK that normally means an overrun system that applies the trailer brakes when the car slows. The car's towing module has to be wired for the trailer's lights and, where fitted, its fridge and charging circuits. On a modern electric car the electrics run through the body control module, so a universal bypass relay spliced into the loom is the wrong approach; a dedicated wiring kit and software coding are what keep the car's systems, including its stability programme and its parking sensors, behaving properly.

That coding point is also a warranty point. An aftermarket towbar fitted without the manufacturer's approved wiring can affect both the warranty on the affected circuits and, in some cases, the car's insurance position. Factory towbar preparation, or an approved retrofit, is the cheaper route over five years even when it looks dearer on day one.

## What towing does to range

This is where electric towing differs most from diesel towing, and where expectations need resetting before the first trip rather than at the first charger.

A car that returns 3.8 miles per kWh solo will typically return somewhere between 1.8 and 2.4 miles per kWh with a caravan on the back at motorway speed. Aerodynamic drag rises with the square of speed and the frontal area of a caravan is roughly that of a van, so the penalty is worst exactly where touring happens. Expect range to fall by 40% to 50%, and expect the fall to be nearer 50% in wind, rain or cold.

The practical consequence is a planning rule: assume 100 miles between charging stops, not 200, and pick the stops before you leave. A 77kWh car that comfortably covers 250 miles unladen may be looking for a charger at 110 miles with a trailer attached. That is not a defect; it is the physics of moving a large box through air, and it applies to diesel tow cars too, just with a denser energy source and a five-minute refuel.

Regenerative braking does help on descents, recovering energy that a diesel would turn into heat, but it is not a substitute for trailer brakes. On a long alpine-style descent the trailer's overrun brakes are doing the work, and the car's regen is a bonus rather than a strategy.

## Charging with a trailer on the back

The single biggest practical friction in electric towing is not range. It is the layout of charging sites.

Most rapid hubs are nose-in or side-on, designed for a car to reverse or pull alongside. With a trailer attached, a nose-in bay becomes unusable and a side-on bay may leave the trailer blocking a lane. Some newer hubs have pull-through bays explicitly for towing vehicles, and motorway service areas often have more space than urban sites, but coverage is patchy and there is no reliable way to filter for it in most apps.

The workaround is to unhitch. That adds ten minutes and a physical chore to every stop, and it is why experienced electric towers plan fewer, longer stops rather than the multiple short ones that suit solo driving. It also argues for charging to a higher state of charge than the 80% rule would suggest, because the next leg is short and the unhitching cost is fixed.

We looked at the hardware side of long-distance charging in [our guide to motorway service chargers](/motorway-service-chargers/), and the general shape of the network in [the state of UK public charging](/uk-charging-network-state/). The direction of travel is toward more pull-through capacity, but a buyer planning to tow this winter should assume they will be unhitching.

## What to check before you buy

Work through the list in order, because each item can rule a car out.

Check the braked towing capacity against the trailer you actually intend to pull, loaded, not unladen. Check the noseweight against the towbar you can fit. Check whether the car offers factory towbar preparation, and what the approved retrofit costs. Check the warranty wording on towing, because some manufacturers limit it. Check the insurance premium with a towbar declared, since it is a modification. And check the charging map for the routes you actually drive, since a car that tows beautifully is no use if the only rapid on your annual route is a nose-in bay with a kerb behind it.

Then accept the range penalty as a planning input rather than a disappointment. Electric towing works, and for the driver doing a handful of caravan trips a year it works well: quiet, stable, cheap to run when charging at home, and with torque that makes a hill start with a trailer feel easy. It is simply a different rhythm of journey, with shorter legs and more deliberate stops, and the drivers who enjoy it are the ones who planned for that before they set off.
